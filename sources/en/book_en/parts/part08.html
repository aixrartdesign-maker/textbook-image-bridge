PART 08

Introduction

Advertising is the hardest form: it must move a stranger's heart in a very short time. The audience didn't come for the ad, and most are waiting for it to end. Having to leave something behind under these hostile conditions is what sets advertising apart from other video.

So advertising has a strict structure: grab attention, make it understood, make it remembered. In 15 seconds, these three stages get 3, 8 and 4 seconds. There must be one message; put in two and both vanish. This is not taste but a rule of thumb proven again and again.

The second half of this PART analyzes the techniques of directors and production companies. Not to copy them, but to understand why they did what they did. Learning starts the moment you pause an ad you like and write down why it cut here. Hitting like is not research.

Background 13

How ads are remembered

An ad's goal is not admiration in the moment of viewing but a memory that surfaces when it's needed. Designing an ad film is, in the end, designing the audience's attention and memory.

One of the oldest models is AIDA, credited to the American adman Elias St. Elmo Lewis in the late 19th century: attention, interest, desire, action. Many object that consumers don't follow these steps in order, but one point still holds: in a short ad, if you don't win attention first, the other stages never begin.

On memory, the German psychologist Hermann Ebbinghaus's forgetting curve is often cited. His experiments in the 1880s showed that newly learned information fades fast, but repetition slows the forgetting. That is why ads are repeated across many media. The mere-exposure effect Robert Zajonc described in 1968, the tendency to like what we see often, belongs to the same picture.

In How Brands Grow (2010), marketing scholar Byron Sharp described the key to brand growth as 'mental availability': how easily a brand comes to mind in a buying situation. Distinctive assets such as color, logo, sound, character and slogan must be repeated consistently, and the brand clearly linked within the ad. If an ad was fun but nobody remembers whose it was, it may have helped another brand.

Daniel Kahneman described human thinking as a fast, intuitive System 1 and a slow, analytical System 2. Short video ads work mostly on System 1: viewers take them in through feeling and impression rather than logic. The 'peak-end rule', that the most intense moment and the last moment of an experience shape the whole memory, is a good guide for designing an ad's climax and ending.

The hooks, message design, structures by length and brand imprinting in this chapter all rest on these principles of memory. When planning an ad, ask 'what will stay in the viewer's head a week later?' before 'what should we show?' Further reading — Byron Sharp, How Brands Grow; Daniel Kahneman, Thinking, Fast and Slow; Robert Cialdini, Influence.

Background 14

The logic of copyright

Borrowing is not making. Copyright is not a rule to block creation but a social contract to keep it going. Copyright protects expression, not ideas. This is the idea-expression dichotomy. The idea of 'a story that turns back time' is open to anyone, but a specific work's dialogue, scene construction, and editing rhythm belong to that work. To use a reference means to learn its principles, not to copy its frames.

Korean copyright law separates economic rights from moral rights. Economic rights cover reproduction, performance, public transmission, distribution, the making of derivative works, and more, and they can be transferred. Moral rights, such as the right of attribution and the right of integrity, stay with the creator and cannot be transferred. A commissioning contract may say 'all rights are transferred' and moral rights still remain, so in practice a separate agreement not to exercise them is common.

Video stacks several layers of rights. To use one song, you must clear both the copyright in the music and lyrics and the neighboring rights in the recording. 'I bought the track' usually means a license to play it, which is not the right to put it in a video. Performers' faces and voices carry likeness rights and publicity interests, and a location may need the facility manager's consent. Fonts, logos, even a poster that happens to land in the background can be subject to rights.

Fair use is the clause that handles exceptions. Article 35-5 of the Korean Copyright Act provides that, for uses such as reporting, criticism, education, and research, the purpose and character of the use, the type of work, the share of the work used, and the effect on the market be weighed together. It is not an automatic exemption but a standard judged after the fact. The folk rule that 'up to a few seconds is fine' has no basis. Quote only as much as you need, cite the source, and do not replace the original's market.

Generative AI adds new questions. The legality of training data, the authorship of outputs, and outputs that imitate a particular creator's style are under debate in many countries. Many countries, Korea included, have taken the position that output without human creative contribution can hardly be copyrighted. Make a habit of recording the tools and prompts you used and the extent of human involvement. In this book's plates, too, the diagrams were designed by the author, while the figure drawings and reference images were made with generative AI; the terms are set out in the appendix.

Three habits protect your practice. First, record the source and scope of use for every asset you use. Second, check the contract for the scope, term, media, and territory of the rights. Third, if in doubt, don't use it, or ask permission. Most copyright disputes start not from malice but from missing records. Further reading — Korea Copyright Commission, Copyright Made Easy: 100 Consultation Cases; Copyright Act, Article 35-5; Lawrence Lessig, Free Culture.

Background 15

When technology changed form

New equipment has always produced new images. Here are five such moments. The first is sound. After "The Jazz Singer" in 1927, as sync sound became standard, the noisy camera was shut away in a soundproof booth, and early talkies actually turned visually stiff. Only after the blimp and the boom mic were developed could the camera move again. This order, in which a new technology first constrains expression and later frees it, would repeat from then on.

The second is film speed and lenses. Deep-focus shooting in the 1940s required stopping down for a deep depth of field, which in turn demanded a lot of light and fast film stock. That figures in front and behind are sharp at once in "Citizen Kane" was both an aesthetic choice and a result of technology. The audience chooses where to look, and the characters' relationships are revealed through composition.

The third is the lighter camera. As portable sync-sound equipment spread in the late 1950s, cinéma vérité in France and direct cinema in the US emerged. Once the camera went out into the streets, shaky frames and improvised compositions began to read as the mark of the real. The handheld look of today's variety shows and vlogs inherits that convention.

The fourth is the digital intermediate and color grading. In the early 2000s, as the process of scanning film to digital, grading it, and transferring it back to film took hold, it became possible to redesign a whole scene's color in post-production. This is where color became a work's signature, as with teal-and-orange contrast. Today it is common to set the look at the shooting stage and apply it to the monitors on set.

The fifth is the smartphone and the vertical screen. As shooting, editing, and distribution came to be done on one device, production cycles shortened, and the vertical frame made people-centered close-ups and fast cuts the default. Captions stand in for sound, and the first 3 seconds govern the whole structure. It is not that the aspect ratio changed; the audience's viewing posture and setting changed.

There is a common lesson. Technology does not decide form, but it changes the range of what is possible. Those who explore that range first create the grammar of the next generation. When you read a case, ask not only 'what was used?' but also 'what did that equipment make possible?' Further reading — David Bordwell · Kristin Thompson, Film History: An Introduction; Barry Salt, Film Style and Technology; John Belton, American Cinema/American Culture.

Mise-en-scène

The design of what to put in the frame and how. Some styles reveal it; others hide it.

Park Chan-wook's frames, with left-right symmetry and artificial color, don't hide the fact that 'this image is constructed'. Bong Joon-ho, by contrast, designs frames to look natural but plants meaning in differences of height within the space.

Advertising: the architects of 15 seconds

As a leading example of work that changed how Korean ads speak, we look at Dolphiners Films (director Shin Woo-seok). The premise changes with each campaign, but the structure stays consistent.

  1. 01Builds a world of its own first instead of explaining the product. Lets the audience learn that world's rules.
  2. 02Pushes an unreal premise with a straight face to the end. Half-hearted exaggeration is the most awkward.
  3. 03Repeats one structure, then varies it at the end. Music and cutting rhythm set the timing of the laughs.
  4. 04Plants details in the corners of the frame that invite rewatching. They become reasons to rewatch and share.
  5. 05Doesn't hide that it's an ad; it shows it, turning resistance into laughter.
  6. 06Backs the idea with high production craft. Sloppy execution looks amateur.

A music video can do without narrative, but it has to hold up on rhythm, color, and composition alone. It's the best format for training in compressed film grammar.

Three formulas, read through structure

AKMU · 2026Paradise of Rumors

  • An ensemble of twenty-four people dancing to their own rhythms in one open field
  • The members appear for only about 40 s of 3 min 37 s — the stars step aside and the space becomes the star
  • Not razor-sharp unison but a crowd of mixed personalities — the Background reading's mood, put on screen

  • One dance move became a meme, and imitation drove the spread

BTS · BLACKPINK and the likeThe big-set MV formula

  • Solo cuts per member + group-dance wides + set changes, on repeat
  • Each chorus brings a new set and new colors, so you hear the rhythm with your eyes

Cameras on music shows

Weekly music shows such as Music Bank, Inkigayo and M Countdown run on the grammar of multicamera live broadcasting. A jimmy jib sweeps overhead, a Steadicam slips between formations, and a rail camera runs in the same direction as the choreography. The camera operators' paths are designed together with the choreography once it is set, and in effect become part of it.

As fancams that follow a single member have become content in their own right, stages are now built to serve broadcast cuts and fancam full shots at once. When an ad shoot involves choreography or movement, you can borrow this grammar as is.

Length sets the form

From 6 seconds to 90 minutes, a video's length is not mere duration but the form itself. The shorter it is, the more you boil it down to one message; the longer, the more characters and relationships you need.

Short forms

5–6 sBumperSkippable ads show the skip button after 5 seconds; bumpers run 6 seconds or less and can't be skipped. One scene, one message, a logo. Brand and key image must all appear within 5 seconds.
15 sBasic unitThe standard for TV and online ads. A 3-part structure: hook (0–3 s) → proof (3–12 s) → brand and call to action (12–15 s).
29 sMicro-shortIn under-30-second contests such as the 29-Second Film Festival, a single twist is everything. Setup in one cut, twist in the last 5 seconds.
1 minShort-formThe standard for ads and promo videos. Re-hook every 8–10 seconds to give a fresh reason to watch to the end (see 7.17).

Long forms

3 minBrand filmA common length for institutional promos. One person, one conflict, one resolution. People's stories come before the product.
10–30 sOutdoor LED loopPlays on repeat without sound. The end must flow into the start, and it must read from afar within 3 seconds (see 7.19).
25 minShort filmThe typical length for festival entries. One event in 3 acts, one change in the protagonist. A cast of 3–4 at most is safest.
90 minFeatureMain plot and subplots interweave, and inner change moves with outer events. The benchmark is a screenplay of about 90–110 pages.

7.17

Ads people watch to the end

Designing a 1-minute ad. The retention curve falls over time. Just before it drops, place a device that pulls viewers back, so every moment creates a fresh 'reason to see what's next.'

  1. 01Open question In the first 3 seconds, pose a question and hold back the answer. Give it in the last 10 seconds.
  2. 02Pattern break Every 8–10 seconds, change one of shot size, place, sound or speed. When the same rhythm runs past 20 seconds, drop-off rises.
  3. 03People and faces Faces and reactions hold attention longer than products. Make viewers curious about the character's choice.
  4. 04Proof and numbers Put a demo, numbers or a before-and-after in the middle to promise a payoff for watching to the end.
  5. 05Brand early and often Show it once in the first 5 seconds, once naturally within the story, and once at the end.
TIP

Insert a new device 3 seconds before the steepest drop.

GEICO's "Unskippable" (2015) declared the ad over, then kept rolling as the family held still while the dog devoured the food on the table, turning the skip button itself into the story.

7.19

Designing outdoor LED loops

Outdoor LED screens are seen without sound, by people walking or driving, from afar, over and over. They need a design entirely unlike cinema or phones.

10–30sLoop unit — the end flows into the start
3sTime to read one line of text
5–10%Minimum text size relative to screen height
1–2minLongest time a viewer stays

Five checks

01Does it work on mute?The message must get across without sound. Text: one sentence, readable within 3 seconds.
02Can it be seen from afar?Text at least 5–10% of screen height, high contrast, simple shapes. Thin lines and small type vanish.
03Is every moment a start?Viewers join mid-loop. Show the brand every few seconds, and make the last frame lead into the first.
04Does it use the screen's shape?Corner screens: anamorphic illusion; vertical screens: up-down motion; giant screens: slow movement for scale (see 6.9).
05Safety and regulationsCheck local-government and media-operator playback standards so brightness and flicker don't distract drivers. Avoid sudden flashes and rapid blinking.

A sentence to use on set

Play the final cut on your phone at arm's length, sound off, for just 3 seconds. Whatever remains then is all that will remain on an outdoor screen.
