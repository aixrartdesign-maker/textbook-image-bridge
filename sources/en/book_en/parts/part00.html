Preface — why this book, why now

The video production environment of 2026 is richer than a decade ago by any measure. The device in your pocket shoots 4K, free editing software yields commercial results, and a few sentences conjure scenes that never existed. What the learner needs now is neither equipment nor information. It is order.

That is exactly where the problem begins. When information is scattered without limit, learners cannot tell what to learn first. They pick up color grading before aperture, and download transition effects before understanding the cut. More tools, fewer finished works — the paradox comes from here. Search gives you knowledge, but not the arrangement of knowledge.

Generative AI has widened the gap. The bar for output has dropped; the bar for judgment has risen. The old proposition that stronger tools raise the value of people who know the principles has never been confirmed so plainly.

This book is an attempt to provide that arrangement. From what video is, through language, technique and planning, to media, market and career, it lays one path a person can walk from beginning to end. Each part stands alone so you can start in the middle, and read in order they form a single narrative.

At the same time, this book is intensely practical. The chapter after explaining why shutter speed is fixed gives setting presets by camera model; the chapter after explaining what a storyboard means gives the full notation set and blank templates. Learn principle and execution apart and each is half of itself. The tables and checklists here are not for reading but for opening on set.

Finally, employment gets a part of its own. Most people who pick up this book want to make a living from video. Knowing which companies hire whom, what a portfolio must prove, and how to survive the first three months is a skill too. A textbook that does not teach it is only half honest.

Author's note — on making a model for teaching

Video production grew up as an industry before it was established as a discipline. The field has a vast body of practice, but texts that organize it into a system are rare. Shooting lives in camera manuals, editing in software guides, careers in job-hunting material, and the learner must stitch the pieces together alone. That stitching is hardest for beginners: knowing what to learn first already takes knowledge.

The teaching side is no different. In a field without a standard textbook, lectures depend on personal experience. Experience is vivid but not reproducible. Reproducibility is the minimum requirement of education, and it begins with a shared text.

This book is an attempt to make that text once. The goal is not a finished canon but a floor on which later revisions and rebuttals can stand. So the content was selected by one criterion: will it still hold in five years? Menu locations in particular software and specs of particular cameras appear only minimally; the pages go to the order of judgment instead. Tools get replaced; the order does not.

The structure assumes both the first-time learner and the person teaching them. Each part opens with a narrative introduction for context, arranges the knowledge item by item, and closes with a one-page summary for class. All plates are monochrome, so nothing is lost in black-and-white copies or on low-resolution projectors.

The same reason governs the part on generative AI, which centers on where in the pipeline to place what, rather than how to operate individual services. Tools live short lives; structures live long ones. In an environment where anyone can make a plausible picture, education's share is not the transfer of functions but the training of judgment.

A word on examples. This book reproduces no frame from any existing work. All diagrams were designed from scratch; drawings and reference images were made with generative image models, their generation conditions disclosed in the appendix. A text that teaches how to make good video should not be built on other people's results.

If this book becomes someone's first textbook, someone's syllabus, or lies open on someone's desk on set, it has done its job. Tell me where the explanation stalls and where it did not fit the field; that is where the next edition begins.

Spring 2026, Jia Ahn

How to read this book

THE READER THIS BOOK ASSUMES

You need never have touched a camera. But by the time you close this book, you should be able to take a 15-second ad, a 1-minute short-form video and a 3-minute brand film from planning to delivery on your own. The content was chosen by that standard.

This book is designed to be used, not just read. Each of the 13 chapters is one self-contained class session, and every chapter arranges the same elements in the same order: lesson plan, narrative introduction, concepts, plates, exercise missions, one-page summary. Use only the chapters you need, or go in order for a full semester.

30 questions to ask first

Skim this list before the main text. These are the questions that come up again and again in class and in advising; if one is on your mind now, jump straight to the chapter in parentheses. The book is designed to be read in order or entered from a question.

Q01Should I buy a camera first, or lights?
Lights. On the same budget, an entry camera with lighting beats a high-end camera without it. (PART 4)
Q02Can I make commercial video on a smartphone?
Yes, provided you turn off auto exposure and auto white balance and use an external mic. (3.13)
Q0324fps or 30fps?
24 for emotional ads and brand films; 30 is the safe choice for informational and caption-heavy videos. (3.8)
Q04Do I really need 4K?
Shoot 4K even if the final is FHD. It leaves room for reframing and vertical crops. (3.7)
Q05Is log always better?
Only when you will grade in post. For chroma key shoots it is actually a disadvantage. (3.10)
Q06Which editing program should I learn first?
By domestic hiring standards, the Premiere Pro and After Effects combination. (5.3)
Q07Can I cut in After Effects?
Not recommended. A tool that handles time horizontally and a tool that handles layers vertically have different jobs. (5.5)
Q08My composites keep looking pasted on
Check three things: black level, atmospheric perspective, grain. (5.7)
Q09How far should color grading go?
Match exposure and white balance, match shot to shot, then apply the look. The order matters. (5.8)
Q10Where do I get music?
Subscription license libraries are safest. Keep the purchase record in the project folder. (4.9)
Q11Is a short clip fine, copyright-wise?
No such rule exists. It is infringement regardless of length. (7.7)
Q12I can't draw storyboards. What do I do?
Drawing skill is not required. Boxes and arrows suffice; the notation rules matter more. (2.1)
Q13What do I do when planning stalls?
Write down 20 pain points of the target and pick the ones the product solves. The safest method. (2.5)
Q14Can't I fit two messages into 15 seconds?
Both vanish. Keep one. (7.1)
Q15How do I build the first 3 seconds?
Pick one of the six types, and never open with a logo and a fade. (7.2)
Q16I'm not getting views
Check reach, click-through, retention and engagement, in that order, and the cause narrows. (8.8)
Q17How long should a title be?
40–60 characters, with the key words in the first 25. (8.11)
Q18How many hashtags?
Mix large, medium and small ones, around 10–15. (8.4)
Q19Can I just crop horizontal footage for vertical?
Shoot 4K and mark vertical guides at the shooting stage. The composition itself changes. (6.2)
Q20Where do short-form captions go?
Center, about one third up from the bottom. The top 12% and bottom 20% are covered by UI. (6.3)
Q21Can I make a whole video with AI?
Replace the entire process and consistency collapses. Limit it to three points: ideation, drafts, automation. (8.9)
Q22Do I have to disclose AI use?
Disclosure requirements are growing in competitions and client deliveries. Record your usage in the documents. (8.9)
Q23How many pieces in a portfolio?
5–8. Your weakest piece is read as your level. (10.3)
Q24I have no experience. What do I put in?
Finished personal work and its process material. Commissioned work is not the only kind that counts. (10.3)
Q25How long should a showreel be?
60–90 seconds. Past 2 minutes nobody watches to the end. (10.4)
Q26What gets asked most in interviews?
"Walk us through one portfolio piece." Answer in order: intent, problem, solution, result. (10.6)
Q27Where is a good first job?
An organization small enough to observe the whole process. Your first role is not your final identity. (10.1)
Q28When should I go freelance?
After you have three months of living costs saved and can write a quote and a contract. (10.10)
Q29The revision requests never end
Because the contract did not state the number of revisions. Usually limit it to 2–3. (9.5)
Q30I feel like I've plateaued
Work on your sound. It is the fastest improvement when you've been staring only at the picture. (4.10)
