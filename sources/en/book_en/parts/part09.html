PART 09

Introduction

Making a good video and getting it discovered are different skills. Since YouTube removed the barriers to distribution, the starting point of planning has moved from the idea to discoverability. That is why titles and thumbnails have become part of directing.

Each platform has its own grammar. YouTube runs on search and watch time, Instagram on saves and shares, TikTok on participation and derivative works. Posting the same video unchanged and then remarking that results differ only shows you don't know what each platform rewards.

This part covers everything from writing titles to building hashtags, answering comments, interpreting performance metrics and the basics of running ads. Why does a maker need this? Because the role most in demand in today's job market is the person who makes, posts and reads the numbers.

Background 16

The attention economy and the algorithmic audience

In the platform era, video does not go straight to the audience; it arrives via recommendation algorithms. To understand views, you must first understand a scarce resource: attention. In a 1971 talk Herbert Simon said that 'a wealth of information creates a poverty of attention,' and in the late 1990s Michael Goldhaber and others called this situation the 'attention economy.' Today every video competes for a few seconds of people's time on the same screen.

Platforms leave the refereeing of this competition to algorithms. In 2012 YouTube announced it would shift its recommendation criteria from clicks to watch time, and since then many platforms have weighted behavioral signals such as retention, completion rate, rewatches and shares. The workings are not public and change often, but the principle is clear: videos that people watch to the end, watch again and send to others get shown to more people.

There are two lessons for makers. First, even if the thumbnail and title win the click, retention drops and recommendations shrink when the content breaks the promise. Exaggerated titles gain short-term clicks and lose long-term trust. Second, what matters is the loop of checking drop-off points, early and midway, in the data and applying what you learn to the next video. The metrics reading in chapter 9 is a tool for this loop.

There is a common structure too. The system first shows a video to a small group of users as a test, then widens or narrows its reach based on the response. The signals it reads are how long people watched and whether to the end, whether they saved or shared it, and whether they came back. Views are a result, not a cause. The variables a maker can work with are not views but retention and response rates.

There is one trap. The observation that a metric breaks down once it becomes a target is known as Goodhart's law. Sensational thumbnails and exaggerated titles raise short-term CTR but increase drop-off and erode trust in the whole channel. Metrics are a window onto the audience's response, not the audience itself.

In the algorithm era, the maker's responsibility grows too. Disinformation, violating other people's portrait rights, and passing off generative-AI images as real are not worth any number of short-term views. A good channel does not trick the algorithm; it steadily gives value to a specific audience. In the end, the algorithm follows the behavior of the people who find that value.

Further reading — Tim Wu, The Attention Merchants · Michael Goldhaber, "The Attention Economy and the Net"

Running YouTube

YouTube runs on search and watch time. The sequence: win the click with title · thumbnail, keep the promise with the content, and extend the session with the next video.

Writing titles

Note

Exaggerated clickbait wins early clicks but lowers retention, and in the end cuts impressions.

  1. 01Put the key words within the first 25 characters. On mobile the end gets cut off.
  2. 02Total length 40–60 characters. Past 100, most of it gets cut.
  3. 03Numbers and concrete words beat abstract ones. "3 ways" rather than "good ways."
  4. 04If you're after search traffic, use the exact phrases people actually type into the search bar.
  5. 05Put the brand name at the end. Up front, it reads as an ad and CTR drops.

Descriptions, tags, chapters

  • The first 2–3 lines of the description show in search results and previews. Put the key points and links here.
  • Up to 3 hashtags from the top of the description appear above the title. Put the three most important first.
  • Splitting chapters with timestamps makes videos easier to navigate and raises average view duration.
  • A pinned comment with extra info or a question boosts comment engagement, and linking the next video through end screens and cards lengthens session watch time.

Background 17

What generative AI changes

Before comparing tools, we first map where this technology enters the production process. Generative AI for video has grown along three branches: models that make images from text, video models that make motion from images and text, and audio models that make voices and music. Their roots differ, but they work in similar ways. They learn patterns from vast amounts of data, then produce results that fit the given conditions probabilistically. That is why the same prompt gives a different result every time.

In practice this probabilistic nature shows up in two ways: drafts come very fast, and the same result is hard to get twice. So in the field, AI serves less as the maker of final output than as a tool for early exploration. It suits stages where you need to see many options fast: rough storyboard images, concept art, layout drafts, caption drafts, alternative copy.

Consistency is the biggest challenge. The same character's face shifts slightly from scene to scene, and the lighting in the same space changes from cut to cut. Fixes include feeding in reference images, making a key image first and generating the rest to match it, and correcting in post-production. Decide in advance 'how far AI goes and where people take over,' or the schedule will fall apart.

In the end, the yardstick is problem-solving. AI quickly produces results that are plausible on average, but it cannot answer why this brand must say this message now, or what the audience should feel in this scene. Judgments of planning, structure and taste still belong to people. The cheaper the tools get, the more the difference lies in the questions of the person using them.

Ethical and legal questions follow too. The source of training data, imitating a particular artist's style, and synthesizing a real person's face or voice all need careful handling. On a commercial project, check the terms of use for whether commercial use is allowed and who owns the rights, and tell the client in advance how far AI will be used. When you use a person's face or voice, always get that person's consent.

In short, this technology does not remove a stage of production; it sharply lowers the cost of a first draft at every stage. What grows is not slack but options. The skilled people of this era are those who know what to choose from a bigger pile of drafts.

Good and bad uses of language models

Effective uses

  • Expanding ideas — set conditions, get 20, narrow to 3
  • Script draft — set the structure first, then ask for the flesh
  • Copy variations — ask for 10 versions of one approved line
  • Organizing research — paste in what you gathered and ask for structure
  • Feedback — paste in your proposal and ask for its weak points
  • Translation — supply context and a glossary

What not to do

  • Taking the first answer as is
  • Asking it to write everything from a blank page
  • Handing over the concept itself
  • Quoting without fact-checking
  • Stopping at praise
  • Using jargon unchecked

Sample AI tool workflow

Note

A person writes the one-sentence concept. This step is never delegated.

  1. 01Use a language model to generate a target persona and 20 pain points, then narrow them to 3 yourself.
  2. 02From the narrowed ideas, a person writes the one-sentence concept.
  3. 03Make mood boards and storyboard drafts with an image generator. Agreeing on tone and manner gets much faster.
  4. 04Generate only the inserts you cannot shoot live. Fill the whole piece with generated footage and consistency collapses.
  5. 05Make a draft narration with speech synthesis to test length and rhythm. For the final, replace it with a voice actor or your own recording.
  6. 06Make 1st-pass subtitles automatically, then have a person check proper nouns and spelling.
  7. 07Log AI use in the production documents. Contests and client deliveries increasingly require disclosure.

What AI cannot replace

The judgment to decide what to shoot, the language to explain why it has to be that cut, the coordination to reach agreement with the client, the decisions that handle surprises on set. The more powerful the tools, the more judgment is worth. Entry-level hiring has begun to value explaining your reasoning over tool skills.

Three rules for mixing generated cuts with live action

Facesoff-limitsNot for cuts where a face is large in frame
1/3maxKeep generated cuts to 1/3 of a piece or less
Lens+grainLay the live cuts' lens distortion and grain over the generated cuts

Five rules for writing prompts

  • Write in this order: subject, action, place, time, lighting, lens, mood. The more specific, like a shooting brief, the more stable the result.
  • Positive phrasing works better than negative. Write "an empty street," not "no people."
  • Use camera terms. Most models have learned words like wide-angle, telephoto, low angle and shallow depth of field.
  • Don't ask for text in the image. Adding lettering yourself in the edit is always more accurate.
  • Running the same prompt several times and choosing is faster than struggling to write the perfect prompt in one go.

One-page summary

PART 08 The first 3 seconds of an ad

Tell apart the six types of 3-second hook, and be able to explain which type a favorite ad's first 3 seconds use.

8.1 Platform map

Whether viewers came to watch or ran into it while watching something else decides the first-3-second strategy.

8.2 Running YouTube

Keywords in the title's first 25 characters; thumbnail text 3–7 characters, at least 15% of screen height.

8.3 The big 3 short-form platforms

Shorts 20–60 s, Reels 15–30 s, TikTok 9–21 s. The top 12% and bottom 20% are hidden by the UI.

8.4 Running Instagram

10–15 hashtags, mixing large · medium · small. Reply to comments in the first hour after posting.

8.5 TikTok and remixing

Make it perfect and no one will touch it. Room to imitate is what makes it spread.

8.7 Blog platforms

Put summary text above and below the video and move the script into the body, and search traffic rises.

8.8 Key performance indicators

Read the metrics in order: reach, click-through rate, retention, engagement.

8.9 Generative AI tools

Limit AI to three points: expanding ideas, visualizing drafts, automating post. Never delegate the one-sentence concept.

8.10 Running a channel

One-sentence definition, fixed formats, upload schedule, visual rules, one metric. Reuse one idea across many media.

8.12 SNS ads

Run 3–5 creatives at once, compare one variable at a time, and always label sponsorships · ads.

8.13 AI pipeline

Keep generated cuts off faces and to 1/3 or less, and lay live action's lens distortion and grain over them. Log AI use.

Field tips

Response in the first hour after posting drives later reach, so post at a time when you can answer comments. Always have a person check proper nouns in auto subtitles. One misspelled company name wrecks trust in an instant. Log AI use in the production documents. More and more commissions come with a disclosure duty.

Questions to consider

Can I define my channel in one sentence? Is the reason to subscribe in that sentence?
It is the first of the 5 channel-design steps in 8.10. If you can't say what the channel gives, and to whom, there is no reason to subscribe.
What part of my work can AI not do for me?
The judgment to decide what to shoot, the language to explain why it has to be that cut, coordination with the client, handling surprises on set. The more powerful the tools, the more judgment is worth.
