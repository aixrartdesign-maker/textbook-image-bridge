PART 11

Introduction

Skill is only skill once it's proven. However well you make something, if you keep it in a form you can't show, it might as well not exist. This part covers the craft of proof.

A recruiter spends 1 to 2 minutes on a portfolio on average. In that short time, they want to know just one thing: if we give this person the work, will they see it through? One finished piece answers that better than one flashy cut. Few applicants submit their proposal and storyboard with the work, so simply showing the process sets you well apart.

The same logic is why interviews and communication get their own sections. Video work looks solitary, but it is really a job of coordinating all day long. The habits of repeating instructions back, sharing progress first, and reporting mistakes immediately are judged before technique. Most of what gets you through your first 3 months at your first job lies here too.

Background 20

What outlasts the tools

Video tools change every few years. The menus of the software you are learning now may well change by the time you graduate. The American radio host Ira Glass has spoken of the 'taste gap' that people starting out in creative work go through. When you start, your taste is already good enough to recognize good work, but your hands can't yet keep up. The only way to close the gap is to keep turning out a large volume of work.

Tool skills matter, but what recruiters and clients finally look at is judgment. Someone who can explain which cuts they dropped, why they chose this angle, and what they prioritized within the deadline and budget adapts quickly when the tools change. That is why a portfolio should hold not just the work but the process and the reasons for your choices.

Video is almost always made collaboratively. The DP, editor, sound designer, client, and actors each speak their own language. A good video maker can translate their intent into other people's language, and takes feedback as information, not emotion. Meeting deadlines, organizing files, and replying when promised: these basics bring the next job.

And what remains in the end is perspective. Given the same tools and the same place, each person sees something different and wants to say something different. In an age when generative AI produces images with ease, this difference matters even more. Anyone can borrow technology, but no one can borrow the ability to judge what is good and say why you are making it.

A useful concept for the first few years of a career is 'T-shaped expertise.' The vertical stroke is the strength to go deep in one field; the horizontal stroke is the breadth to understand neighboring fields and collaborate. An editor who knows the limits of shooting can judge what extra footage to ask for on set, and a DP who knows how the edit flows secures the needed cuts in advance. What a newcomer needs is to do one thing well while understanding the fields beside it.

Remember, too, how to handle feedback. As a beginner, it's easy to take criticism of your work as a judgment of you. But feedback on set is usually information about the output, and the sooner you get information, the better. People who can explain 'why I did it this way' and ask 'what to change, and how' grow far faster in the same time. The hands-on preparation in chapter 11 is, in the end, practice in this attitude.

10.3

Building a portfolio

5–8 works, the strongest first, with role, process, and numbers for each. A portfolio is judged by impression, not by sum.

Seven principles of structure

Note

List 20 pieces and you are judged on the average. Your weakest piece reads as your level.

  1. 015–8 pieces is the right number.
  2. 02The first piece is everything. Recruiters watch for only 1–2 minutes on average. Put your strongest work first.
  3. 03Leave out work unrelated to the role. There is no reason to include photography when applying as an editor.
  4. 04State the role, duration, tools, and purpose for each piece.
  5. 05Show the process too. Proposals, storyboards, set photos, and before-and-after comparisons are stronger evidence than the result.
  6. 06If there are results, add numbers. Views, retention rate, and client response all count.
  7. 07Put recent work up front. If your latest piece is from 3 years ago, it reads as if you've done nothing lately.

Five types you must have

01One complete ad-style piece15 or 30 seconds. Work that shows intent and structure. Submit with proposal and storyboard
02Short-form series3+ episodes in the same format. Proof you can keep a consistent format
03Motion graphicsTitles, infographics, 3D text. Direct proof of After Effects skill
04Interview or documentaryProves basics like lighting, mics, and captions all at once
05Personal workMade without a commission. Shows taste and initiative, and gives you something to talk about in interviews

Background 21

What a portfolio proves

A portfolio is not a collection of well-made videos but a bundle of evidence showing what problems you solve and how. Recruiters do not spend long on a showreel. Surveys and field experience agree that whether they keep watching is decided within the first 10 to 20 seconds. Ordering work chronologically or saving your best for later almost always costs you.

According to the serial position effect in cognitive psychology, people remember the beginning and end of a list best. Portfolios follow the same principle. Open with strong work, show range in the middle, and close with strong work again. Padding with ordinary work to add volume lowers the average. A portfolio is judged by impression, not by sum.

What to show depends on the role. Camera applicants must prove light and composition; editing applicants, rhythm and structure. From the same project, pick the passages that reveal transitions and pacing for an editing role, and the scenes that show lighting design for a camera role. Applying to every role with one showreel is like wearing an outfit that fits no occasion.

Showing the process makes the decisive difference. Note briefly your intent, references, storyboard, the constraints on set, and the choices you made within them, and reviewers will read the judgment behind the result. Stories of handling limits, such as a scene solved with natural light for lack of budget or an edit restructured because the schedule slipped, leave an especially strong impression.

Stating your contribution accurately is both ethics and strategy. Clearly noting your part in team work earns trust and makes interview answers easier. Inflated contributions usually come out after one or two specific questions. Even for school or personal projects, if you used commercial assets or music without permission, it is safer to keep them out of a public portfolio.

Finally, a portfolio is not an object you finish but a state you keep updating. Set yourself a rule to drop the weakest piece whenever a new one arrives, and the average keeps rising even if the volume doesn't.

10.7

Work attitude and communication

Video work is a job of coordinating all day long. The habits of repeating back, sharing first, and reporting at once are judged before technique.

  1. 01Arrive before the agreed time. One person's lateness can wreck the whole schedule.
  2. 02If you don't know, ask right away. Cleaning up what you did without knowing costs far more.
  3. 03Repeat instructions back to confirm them. Ten people will read "grade it a bit warmer" ten different ways.
  4. 04Share progress first. Whoever speaks up before being asked earns trust.
  5. 05Report mistakes immediately. A late report becomes a bigger problem than the mistake.
  6. 06Keep files and folders organized. A structure anyone else can open and understand is the professional standard.
  7. 07Handle equipment with care. How you treat the gear reads as how you treat the work.

Giving and receiving feedback

When receiving feedback

  • Hear it out first
  • Ask to confirm instead of making excuses
  • Repeat back what to fix and by when
  • Separate your feelings from the work
  • After fixing, report what you changed

When giving feedback

  • Be specific, down to the timecode
  • No "it's lacking overall"
  • Say what works first
  • Offer an alternative
  • Don't single people out in public
TIP

Exchanging notes by timecode cuts revision time in half.

Sample feedback notes

0:04 caption appears 0.3 s late / 0:12 cut comes after the action ends, drags / 0:19 face is dark, raise gamma / 0:27 logo on screen 1 s too short.

10.10

Freelancing and startups

Freelancers run on trust more than skill. The next job goes to whoever meets deadlines.

Note

Revision count and usage scope: these two cause 90% of disputes. A verbal agreement carries no weight in a dispute.

  1. 01Your starting rate sticks for a long time. Start too low and it is hard to raise.
  2. 02Always use a quote and a contract, and put revision count and usage scope in the contract.
  3. 03Check taxes, social insurance and business registration in advance. Once income passes a certain level, you must sort them out.
  4. 04Before going full-time, set aside 3 months of living costs for slow periods.
  5. 05Run a personal channel or blog on the side and inquiries come in at no sales cost.

Online communication and remote collaboration

Much of the job happens over messenger and email. Even a well-made video loses points if it is handed over clumsily.

Basic format for sending work

Hello, this is OOO. Here is the 1st cut of the 15-second ad you requested. 1. Link: (URL) no password, downloadable 2. Changes made: logo timing changed per our last meeting, caption font replaced 3. Please check: whether the music transition at 0:08 works 4. Next step: 2nd cut within 2 days of your feedback. Thank you.

TIP

Give the next step up front. Not keeping people waiting is the heart of trust.

  • Always check that a link opens in an incognito window before sending it.
  • Spell out what you want checked. With only "please review," the other side won't know what to look at.
  • Put the project name, version and date in the file name.
  • Reply to revision requests organized by timecode. Anything traded verbally will get lost.

TIP

Views, retention, follower growth, number of videos made and time saved are all valid numbers.

  • Attach process materials. Few applicants submit proposals and storyboards, so that alone sets you apart.

  6. 06Speak up at least once in every meeting. Quiet new hires are treated kindly but not remembered.

The fastest route with no experience

Don't wait for commissions; create your own clients. Make videos with a real purpose, for a local shop, a department event or a friend's work, and they become your track record. Professional experience is defined not by whether you were paid but by whether you made it for someone's purpose.

10.16

Beyond the tools

What will you say? This book has spent hundreds of pages on technique, but one sentence should remain at the end: an editing tool is only a tool.

Software changes its interface within 3 years, and within 5 a new tool replaces the standard. The shortcuts you know by heart today will be strange relics to the next generation. But the ability to decide what to show, to explain that choice and to design where the audience's eye goes keeps accumulating.

Technique wears out; judgment builds up.10.16 Beyond the tools

Generative AI has made this distinction sharper. The cheaper it gets to make a plausible image, the more valuable the person who can say why it must be this image. Having something to say has become harder than learning tools fast.

The thesis this book has carried throughout

Video is not technique but language. Learning a language is not memorizing grammar; it is saying exactly what you mean. We learn aperture not to get exposure right but to decide where the audience looks. The moment that order flips, we may become technicians, but never creators.

10.17

The age of the one-person company

And the power of culture. Creators no longer just make work; they run themselves as a business. And the accumulation of that skill becomes the power of culture.

Long ago, Lee O-young said an age was coming when each individual would become a company. Then it was a metaphor; now it is a description. Planning, shooting and editing on one smartphone and distributing worldwide is now a reality. That is why this book covers not only planning and production but platform management, reading results, contracts and careers.

In Baekbeom Ilji, Kim Gu wrote that what he wanted was not a rich and powerful nation but one with a high power of culture, because the power of culture makes us happy and brings happiness to others. It was a bold declaration, setting culture as a national goal at a time with neither military nor economic might.

Remember

This is not the achievement of a few geniuses. It is the accumulated skill of tens of thousands, and that skill begins with education.

Korean video content now stands where that sentence has come true. It wins at Cannes and the Academy Awards, sets global streaming viewing records, and its fusion of music and video has become one of the world's standards. The readers of this book will supply that skill for the next generation. Getting exposure right, recording clean sound, cutting for a reason, meeting deadlines. That accumulation becomes the power of culture.
