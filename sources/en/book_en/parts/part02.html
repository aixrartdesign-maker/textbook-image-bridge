PART 02

Introduction

The moment we turn on the camera, we make three decisions at once: where to stand, what to include and how much, and how long to show it. Together these decisions become meaning. What separates good video from bad is mostly not the gear but the precision of these decisions.

The language of video works like grammar. A close-up demands emotion; a long shot explains the situation. A low angle makes the subject big; a high angle makes it small. This is not taste but a convention audiences have learned over 100 years. Know the conventions and you can convey information without explanation; once you know them, you can also break them on purpose. Break rules you don't know and it just reads as a mistake.

Everything in this part can be practiced today, even without gear. Shooting one person in eight sizes on a single smartphone, and shooting the same conversation from two angles to compare: these are the real goals of this chapter. Knowing from reading and knowing from shooting are entirely different kinds of knowledge.

Background 2

How film grammar was made

Shots, cuts and angles were not rules fixed from the start. Conventions early filmmakers discovered one by one, watching how audiences reacted, piled up into today's film grammar.

The Lumière brothers' first screenings in 1895 were mostly single-shot films from a fixed camera. The camera stayed in one place, like a seat at a play. In the same period, Georges Méliès used the trick of stopping the camera and starting it again to show that film could not only record reality but create a new one. Record and fantasy: these two branches live on today as documentary and VFX.

Telling a story by joining cuts developed fast in the early 1900s. Edwin Porter's The Great Train Robbery (1903) joined events in different places into a single chase. D. W. Griffith made systematic use of cross-cutting, close-ups that heighten emotion, and a rhythm that varies cut length with the pace of events. But his The Birth of a Nation (1915) is still sharply criticized for its racist content. Innovation in form does not guarantee the legitimacy of content.

Soviet filmmakers gave editing even more weight. Lev Kuleshov presented an experiment showing that when the same actor's blank face is joined to different scenes, audiences read entirely different emotions. Sergei Eisenstein argued that the collision of two cuts produces a new meaning found in neither, and divided montage into metric · rhythmic · tonal · intellectual montage. Today's ads that rapidly intercut a product with symbolic images stand on this tradition.

The Hollywood studio system, by contrast, chose editing that goes unnoticed. Rules that keep audiences from confusing space and time, like the 180-degree rule, eyeline match and match on action, were refined, and David Bordwell summed this up as the classical Hollywood style. Most of the continuity editing rules in this PART come from here.

In 1960, Jean-Luc Godard's Breathless deliberately broke these rules with jump cuts that skip time within the same framing. The jump cut, so common on YouTube today, was once revolutionary grammar. Film grammar is not a fixed code of law but a language audiences and makers have agreed on together, rewritten a little with every new platform.

Further reading — David Bordwell · Kristin Thompson, Film Art; Sergei Eisenstein, Film Form; Louis Giannetti, Understanding Movies

1.1

Shot, scene, sequence: the units of video

Video has a hierarchy, like sentences. Mix up scene and cut in a storyboard meeting and the shooting schedule itself goes wrong.

Term

By convention, it is called a shot during shooting and a cut during editing.

FrameFrame

  • A single still image; the smallest unit of editing and timecode
  • At 24fps, 1 second = 24 frames

ShotShot

  • Footage recorded without a break from when the camera rolls until it stops

SceneScene

  • Events grouped by same place · same time
  • A change of place or time means a new scene; the basic unit of a screenplay

SequenceSequence

  • A group of scenes leading toward one purpose
  • A whole chase, preparation or flashback

TakeTake

  • Each repeated attempt at the same shot
  • Take 3 is the third attempt

CoverageCoverage

  • How fully a scene is secured in different sizes and angles
  • Too little leaves no options in the edit

On-set notation example

S#12 / C#04 / T3 means scene 12, cut 4, take 3. Just writing this correctly on the slate cuts editing time in half.

Background 3

How does the eye read the screen?

The rules of composition come from human visual perception before they are art-textbook conventions. Know how the viewer's eye moves, and composition stops being a formula to memorize and becomes a tool you can design with.

In the early 20th century, the German Gestalt psychologists Max Wertheimer · Wolfgang Köhler · Kurt Koffka argued that we don't add up parts to see a whole; we grasp the whole first. Things close together are seen as a group (proximity), similar things as belonging together (similarity), broken lines as continuous (continuity), and open shapes are completed as closed ones (closure). Put two people close together in frame and the audience reads them as being on the same side, no explanation needed.

In "Rubin's vase," introduced by Edgar Rubin in 1915, the same lines read as a vase or as two faces looking at each other. The eye makes only one thing the figure at a time. Controlling focus, brightness and color contrast in video is ultimately telling the audience what the figure is right now. Blurring the background with shallow depth of field and lighting only the person work on the same principle.

In Art and Visual Perception, Rudolf Arnheim described invisible forces at work in the frame. A rectangle's center, corners and diagonals hold a tension that pulls the eye, and the further an object strays from center, the less stable it feels. That balance of forces is why the rule of thirds feels comfortable and a figure pressed against the edge feels cramped. Composition is not finding the right answer but adjusting how much tension and stability there is.

In eye-tracking experiments in the 1960s, Alfred Yarbus showed that the eye moves over the same picture in entirely different ways depending on the question the observer is given. Asked to estimate the people's ages, observers look at faces; asked about their finances, at clothes and furniture. The audience's gaze follows not just shapes but the questions the story asks. Plant the question "who is coming in?" in one cut, and in the next the audience looks at the door first.

So composition and eye guidance can't be separated from editing. Put the next cut's key subject where the eye came to rest in the last, and the cut flows; put it deliberately on the opposite side, and you get a jolt. Whenever you meet a rule in this chapter, also ask: "where is the audience's eye right now?"

Further reading — Rudolf Arnheim, Art and Visual Perception; Bruce Block, The Visual Story; Margaret Livingstone, Vision and Art

Basic composition principles

BasicRule of thirds

  • Main subject where the 3-way grid lines cross
  • Most stable, low risk of failure

DeclarativeCentral symmetry

  • Dead center; declarative and strong
  • Logo cuts, frontal interviews, authority

CurveGolden ratio

  • A spiral draws the eye inward
  • Natural forms, curved subjects

MotionDiagonal

  • Lines across the frame create motion
  • Gives speed to a static product

StabilityTriangle

  • Three elements in a triangle
  • Three-shots, product displays

HorizonHorizon placement

  • Top 1/3: stresses the ground
  • Bottom 1/3: sky and openness

Three names for space

Note

In ads, negative space is where captions and logos go. Secure it before the shoot; it can't be made in post-production.

  • Headroom — space above the head. Too much and the person sinks; too little and it feels cramped. In a close-up, cropping above the forehead actually looks more natural.
  • Nose room (looking room) — space in front of the gaze. If there's more space behind the gaze, the audience feels uneasy for no clear reason.
  • Lead room — space ahead of a moving subject. Too little and it seems about to hit a wall.
  • Negative space — space left empty on purpose. It's where captions and logos go.

Advanced composition techniques

  • Frame within a frame — use door frames, windows, mirrors or fingers to make a second frame inside the frame.
  • Linear perspective — depth from the vanishing point of roads, corridors, stairs.
  • Repetition and pattern — repeat a shape for rhythm and make one different.
  • Foreground objects — put leaves, glass or fabric in front of the lens to add layers. Depth on a low budget.
  • Broken symmetry — knock one element out of a perfect symmetry and the eye is forced there.
  • Layering — place information in foreground, midground and background to pack more into one cut.

1.8

The 180° rule and the 30° rule

These two rules are the minimum agreement that keeps the audience oriented in space. Only those who know the rules can break them.

The 180° rule (the line)

TIP

When you must cross the axis, go via a neutral head-on cut, a cut where the camera moves across, or an insert.

Draw an imaginary line between two people or objects and shoot from one side of it only. Cross it and the people swap sides, the dialogue falls apart, and the audience loses its sense of space.

  • Break this rule in a sports broadcast and a team seems to attack the wrong way. That's why soccer broadcast cameras sit on one side only.
  • The fewer the cuts, as in a 15-second ad, the more one violation wrecks the whole. Draw a floor plan at the storyboard stage and fix camera positions in advance.

Eyeline match: three checks

  1. 01When two people look at each other — are the eyelines at the same height and in opposite directions? If A looks right, B must look left.
  2. 02When someone looks into the camera — is the lens at their eye height? Higher or lower, and they look down or up at the viewer instead of at them.
  3. 03When someone looks behind or off screen — is the next cut the space in that direction? If the gaze exits left, the next cut must be what was on the left.

The line and eyeline match are the same agreement. 1.18 Eyeline direction picks this up with five kinds of gaze.

The 30° rule

When you shoot the same subject again, change the camera angle by at least 30°. Any less and the two cuts look nearly the same and read as a jarring jump cut. Changing size by two steps as well is safer still.

Only those who know the rules can break them. Short-form jump cuts flatly violate the 30° rule but are allowed because they serve a clear purpose: pace.1.8 The 180° rule and the 30° rule

The meaning color makes

Color is read first on screen and noticed last. Before reading a single caption, the audience has already judged whether the scene is warm or cold.

TIP

Think of color as setting the direction of emotion, and saturation its intensity.

  • Color is decided on set. If wall, costume and prop colors aren't settled in advance, post-production can't undo it.
  • Always plant the brand color somewhere in the frame. Change color in post-production and skin tones shift with it.
  • Keep one color temperature within a scene. Mixed light multiplies grading time in post-production.
  • If you plan to extend into outdoor and print, allow in advance for highly saturated colors not reproducing.

1.15

Point of view: whose eyes are we looking through?

The camera is always placed in someone's position. Choosing that position is point of view, and when point of view wavers, the audience falls out of the story.

ObserverObjective POV

  • Eye-level medium shot by default, information kept neutral
  • Most informational videos and interviews

Character's eyesSubjective POV

  • Needs a cut just before it showing the character looking
  • The basis of horror and immersion

In betweenOver-the-shoulder POV

  • Shows the relationship, takes one side
  • Standard for dialogue

An impossible vantageOmniscient POV

  • High angle, bird's-eye
  • Situation as diagram; fated distance

Action cam1st-person record POV

  • The vlog's POV
  • Camera shake as proof of reality

Machine's eyeSurveillance POV

  • CCTV, monitors, drone feeds
  • Information gap and unease

Keep the POV consistent within a film

If a subjective POV suddenly cuts into an objective one, the audience reads it as meaningful. Without meaning it is a mistake; with meaning it is a powerful device. You must be able to explain which it is.

Cropping

This section covers the meaning made outside the frame. In video, what is not seen is not absent; it has been cut off. What you push out of the frame matters as much as what you keep in.

Note

Don't crop people at the joints. Cutting at the wrist, elbow, knee or ankle makes it feel as if the cut-off part has been left outside the frame.

  • Offscreen sound builds a world beyond the frame. An unseen character's voice gives that character a larger presence.
  • Cropping part of the product in a product ad is risky. If consumers can't make out its shape, they won't buy it.
  • If you plan to reuse the footage vertically, shoot assuming the sides will be cut. Keep what matters on the central vertical axis.

1.17

Angles on the face

A face seen from above and one seen from below belong to different people. In a close-up, even a slight change in camera height shifts the face's proportions, and those proportions are read as character. The audience takes in the shape of a face before its expression.

Reference photos 1.17AGenerated image — directing reference

Rules on set

TIP

A face's angle is read before its expression. Before changing the performance, try changing the camera height.

  • Measure camera height against the subject's eyes. A lens just a hand-span below the eyes is already a low angle.
  • The wider the lens, the stronger the angle effect. A 24mm low angle becomes exaggeration; an 85mm low angle becomes restrained authority.
  • Changing a character's angle within a scene marks a shift in power. Start with a high angle, say, then move to a low angle once they have made up their mind.

Eyeline direction: meeting and following

The audience looks where the person on screen looks. A gaze is the strongest directional line in a composition, and in editing it is the thread that ties one cut to the next.

Link

The eyeline match is the promise that the next cut shows where the gaze points. The 180-degree rule in 1.8 is what keeps that direction, and the three checks in 1.8 apply unchanged to these five gazes.

Meeting: put both gazes on one line

  • If A looks screen right, B looks left. The line of the 180-degree rule is the line joining the two gazes
  • Match eye height and gaze angle across the two cuts. If only one is close to the lens, it reads as a monologue
  • A two-shot facing each other means conflict or intimacy; a two-shot looking the same way means companionship and empathy

Following: the gaze calls up the next cut

  • Look, what is seen, reaction: three cuts, the most basic gaze link
  • The Kuleshov effect — the same blank face changes meaning with the next cut. Editing, more than acting, gives a reaction cut its meaning
  • Put brightness · sharpness · movement · faces where the gaze points, and the audience's eyes follow

1.19

Principles of visual form

This is about proportion · balance · rhythm. If composition is a question of where to place things, the principles of form are a question of how to relate them. Read with the composition plates in 1.6.

Term

The rule of thirds (1 : 2) is an approximation that simplifies the golden ratio (about 1 : 1.618) for easy use on set.

Six principles

Size relationProportion

  • Size of part relative to whole
  • e.g. rule of thirds, golden ratio

WeightBalance

  • Visual weight evened out left and right
  • Symmetry is order and ritual; asymmetry is naturalness and tension

AxisSymmetry

  • Both sides alike about a central axis
  • Frontal symmetry and 1-point corridors: auteur signatures

RepetitionRhythm

  • Repeated shapes or intervals
  • The eye stops the moment one breaks

DifferenceContrast

  • Size, tone, color, direction
  • Bigger gap, clearer subject

FocusEmphasis

  • Focuses the above on one point
  • One emphasis per frame

What changes in video

  • Rhythm in video exists on two layers: repetition within the frame and repetition of cuts. Cuts of equal length become a pattern; a sudden shortening becomes tension.
  • Movement keeps shifting the balance. A person walking in can complete an asymmetric frame into symmetry.

Related artists and theory

  • Rudolf Arnheim Art and Visual Perception (1954) — the classic that explains forces and balance in the picture through the psychology of perception
  • Wassily Kandinsky Point and Line to Plane (1926) — the Bauhaus textbook that systematized the elements of form. Revisited in 6.14
  • Henri Cartier-Bresson — set the standard for photographic composition with the decisive moment and geometric framing
  • Yasujiro Ozu, Wes Anderson — directors who built their own visual grammar from low fixed cameras, frontality and strict symmetry
A good frame is not one with many elements but one with clear relationships.1.19 Principles of visual form

1.20

Time and space in video

A cut moves time and place. Where painting and photography hold one place at one moment, video cuts time and space apart and joins them again. Editing is designing these moves so the audience accepts them naturally.

Six ways to handle time

EqualContinuous

  • Story time = screen time
  • Long takes, real-time structure

DefaultEllipsis

  • Cuts out unneeded time
  • The default of all editing

GatherCompression

  • A long span as a run of short cuts
  • Montage sequence

StretchExpansion

  • A brief moment, drawn out
  • Slow motion, one moment from many angles

Go backBackward

  • Flashbacks and recollection
  • A character's memory as structure

At onceParallel

  • Different places, same time, alternating
  • Cross-cutting — the oldest device for building tension

Ways to join space

Term

Creative geography — Kuleshov's experiment. Cuts shot in different places read as one place once joined.

  • The 180-degree rule and the eyeline match bind separately shot cuts into one space.
  • A match cut joins entirely different places through similar shapes. If the shape matches, the audience accepts the leap.
  • When the camera moves within a cut, space changes without editing. A long-take tracking shot makes the audience walk through that space too.

Related theory

  • Eadweard Muybridge, Étienne-Jules Marey — broke motion into units of time with sequential photographs. Where the idea of the moving image begins
  • Sergei Eisenstein — montage theory: two colliding cuts create a new meaning
  • André Bazin — held that long takes and deep depth of field preserve real time and space better than editing
  • Gilles Deleuze Cinema 1: The Movement-Image (1983), Cinema 2: The Time-Image (1985) — thinks film through images of movement and images of time
Before joining two cuts, decide how much time and distance passes between them.1.20 Time and space in video

One-page summary

1.1 Units of video

Don't mix up scene and cut in storyboard meetings. Notation like S#12 C#04 T3 keeps the schedule precise.

1.2 The 8 shots

Shot size is set not by how big the person is but by where the frame cuts.

1.2 · 1.8 Size and angle

Between two consecutive cuts, change the size by two steps or more and the angle by 30 degrees or more.

1.4 Shots by situation

In every scene, always get 1 master shot and at least 2 inserts.

1.6 Negative space

Space has names. If headroom, nose room or lead room is off, the frame feels wrong for no clear reason. Secure space for captions and logos before shooting.

1.7 Guiding the eye

The audience's eye is drawn to brightness, movement, sharpness and faces. Directing is controlling that order.

1.9 Camera movement

Use only one move per cut. Explain the difference between a dolly and a zoom through perspective.

1.10 Cuts and transitions

Professional editors' work is mostly hard cuts. Polish comes from the order of the cuts, not the number of effects.

1.12 · 1.14 Color

Decide color on set. Plant the brand color in a single prop; don't create it in post.

1.18 · 1.20 Gaze, time and space

Put the next cut where the gaze lands, and decide first how much time and distance lies between two cuts.
