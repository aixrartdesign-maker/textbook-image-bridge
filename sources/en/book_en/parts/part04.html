PART 04

Introduction

Lighting and sound are the two most underrated areas, yet they shape the result more than anything. Viewers will sit through slightly soft footage to the end, but they leave muddy-sounding video within 3 seconds. An interview with a dark face reads as amateur work, however good the content.

Fortunately, in both areas understanding counts for more than money. A window and a white foam board solve half of portrait lighting, and bringing the mic to within 30 centimeters of the mouth removes most sound problems. Cheap gear up close always beats expensive gear far away.

This part covers not only how to add light but how to take it away. Half of low-key lighting is stealing light with black cloth, and half of good recording is switching off noise sources. Lighting design is not the craft of making things bright but of deciding where to leave dark.

Background 5

An aesthetic history of light — from chiaroscuro to natural light

The language of film lighting began on painters' canvases. In late-16th-century Italy, Caravaggio painted dramatic scenes by revealing only part of a figure in strong light against darkness. Such strong light-dark contrast is called chiaroscuro, and its extreme form tenebrism. In 17th-century Holland, Rembrandt loved portraits in which light from one side leaves a small triangle on the shadowed cheek, and on today's sets this lighting bears his name.

The 1920 German Expressionist film The Cabinet of Dr. Caligari treated light and dark as a language of the psyche, even painting shadows directly onto the sets. With the emigration of German filmmakers in the 1930s–40s, this aesthetic crossed to Hollywood and carried into film noir's low-key lighting and venetian-blind shadows falling like prison bars. In 1949, DP John Alton described lighting in Painting with Light as a means of expression rather than a technique.

At the same time, the studio system set the standard for making stars look beautiful. Three-point lighting — key, fill, and backlight — became the formula for separating a person from the background and reliably modeling the face, and it is still the basis of interview and commercial shoots. Italian Neorealism and the French New Wave, by contrast, took light cameras and fast film into the streets, shooting in natural light and choosing realism over artificial beauty.

Today's DPs build their own language between these two traditions. Vittorio Storaro gave color and light symbolic meaning, while Roger Deakins is known for a naturalism that designs light around sources that could plausibly exist in the scene, such as windows or lamps. Digital sensors and LED lights let us shoot with less light, but the principles that govern direction, quality, and contrast have not changed.

When learning lighting, remember that every light has a motivation. Where does this light come from? Why this direction? What does the audience feel from it? The lighting patterns and gear in chapter 4 are tools for answering these questions.

Further reading — John Alton, Painting with Light · Blain Brown, Motion Picture and Video Lighting · E. H. Gombrich, The Story of Art

Five properties of light

To work with light, you must first be able to name it in five words. Quality · direction · color · intensity · contrast, and movement.

Term

Hard light casts sharp-edged shadows; soft light casts gentle ones. The larger and closer the source, the softer the light.

QualityQuality

  • Hard: sharp and dramatic
  • Soft: gentle and forgiving
  • Larger, closer sources are softer

DirectionDirection

  • Front · side · back · top · bottom
  • Direction creates form
  • Side light reveals texture best

ColorColor

  • Color temperature and tint
  • Warm · cool sets time of day and mood
  • Always unify mixed light

IntensityIntensity

  • Amount of light
  • Inversely proportional to distance squared
  • Twice as far, 1/4 the light

ContrastContrast

  • Ratio of bright to dark
  • Lighting ratio sets genre and emotion

MovementMovement

  • Whether the light changes
  • Flicker · passing · reflected motion brings the frame to life

Using the inverse square law in practice

Put the light close to the person and the background darkens sharply; put it far away and person and background come out similar in brightness. To drop the background into darkness, don't buy more lights — pull the source in toward the person.

Three-point lighting and its variations

Know the roles of the three lights — key · fill · backlight — and the formula for lighting people comes into view. Background lights and eye lights extend it.

Reference photos 4.2AGenerated images — staging reference

Main lightKey light

  • The most important light; sets form and shadow
  • Start 45° left or right of camera, 45° up

Support lightFill light

  • Controls the depth of the key's shadows
  • Must be weaker than the key — kill the shadows and depth goes too

Rear lightBacklight

  • Rims the subject to separate it from the background
  • Also called rim light · hair light

Fourth lightBackground light

  • Lights the background separately for depth
  • 3-point lighting, extended

Eye reflectionEye light

  • Eyes without catchlights look lifeless
  • One reflector fixes it
TIP

The fill doesn't have to be a separate light. One white reflector is often enough.

Reflectors and bounce — fill without a light

  1. 01The surface color is the light's character. White gives soft, natural fill; silver, bright and hard fill; gold, warm sunset light; black is negative fill that takes light away.
  2. 02Distance sets softness. The closer the reflector to the subject, the larger the source and the softer the shadow edge.
  3. 03Negative fill subtracts light instead of adding it. A black board on the shadow side brings back contrast and slims the face.
  4. 04Check the catchlights. Tilt the reflector up from below eye level and a small point of light appears in the pupils.
  5. 05Fix outdoor backlight. Put the sun behind the subject and fill the face with a reflector: you get a rim light on the hair and a well-exposed face at once.
  6. 06For two people, put the reflector in the middle. In a face-to-face interview, one white board at center, opposite the key, fills both faces at once.

4.3

Key light in brief

Six rules for when time is short. Try to finish the frame with the key light alone. If it doesn't look good now, adding light won't make it better.

  1. 01Set the key light first. Turn off every other light and try to finish the frame with the key alone.
  2. 02Start at 45° and 45°. The reference point is where the nose shadow makes a small triangle on the far cheek.
  3. 03Keep the source big and close. Enlarge it with a softbox or diffuser and bring it as near the subject as you can.
  4. 04Don't erase the shadows. Fill light is a tool for controlling depth, not for wiping out shadow.
  5. 05Separate with a backlight. If subject and background merge, add one high, opposite the key.
  6. 06Put light in the eyes. It's the item most often missed in ads with people, and one reflector fixes it.
Note

Rembrandt, 45° side-high, is the default for people. Other patterns move the source from this point.

4.4

High key and low key

Low key isn't about shooting dark. It's about exposing the bright parts precisely and controlling the rest into darkness.

Reference photos 4.4AGenerated images — staging reference

High-key lighting

  • Bright overall, faint shadows
  • Ratio 2:1 or less; background lit too
  • Mood: bright, clean, safe, cheerful
  • Use: food, kids, household, medical
  • Note: keep depth with a backlight

Low-key lighting

  • Darkness dominates; only parts lit
  • Ratio 8:1 or more; dark background
  • Mood: luxury, tension, mystery, focus
  • Use: liquor, cars, perfume, electronics
  • Note: underexposure isn't low key
Term

Negative fill — taking light away with black cloth and black foam board. It's half of low-key lighting.

TIP

Fingerprints and dust eat the most post-production time. Clean up with microfiber and an air blower before shooting.

  • Kill unwanted reflections with black cloth and make the bright surfaces you want with white cards. Product shooting is as much taking light away as adding it.

Safety rules

Keep plenty of distance between hot lights and paper or cloth, and always put sandbags on light stands. The most common accidents in class shoots are toppled lights and burns.

Background 6

How sound changes the image

We say we 'watch' video, but in fact we build one experience by seeing and hearing. In Audio-Vision (1990), the French composer and theorist Michel Chion proposed the idea of 'added value': when sound is added to an image, the audience mistakes its effect for the power of the image itself. The sound effect as a fist lands makes a far stronger impact than the real blow, yet the audience remembers the picture as intense. That is why good sound design goes unnoticed.

Chion also coined 'synchresis': the perceptual tendency to weld a sound and an image that occur together into one event. It's why a Foley artist can snap celery to make the sound of a breaking bone. Recording footsteps or the brush of a collar separately is called Foley after Jack Foley, who pioneered the field.

Sound divides into sound with a source inside the story world and sound without one. Radio music a character hears is inside the story; background music only the audience hears comes from outside it. The moment we learn that what we took for score is actually playing in a character's earphones, the audience steps into that character's inner world.

Editor and sound designer Walter Murch was credited as 'sound designer' on Apocalypse Now (1979), raising sound to a creative field with the same weight as cinematography or editing. He stressed designing the mix around the limit of how many overlapping layers of sound an audience can tell apart in one scene. Choosing what to keep matters more than putting a lot in.

In short-form and on mobile, many viewers watch with the sound off. The design has to work on captions and picture alone, yet for viewers with sound on, the first 1 second of sound can be the strongest hook. The mics and sound design of chapter 4 start from keeping both audiences in mind.

Further reading — Michel Chion, Audio-Vision · David Sonnenschein, Sound Design · Walter Murch, In the Blink of an Eye

TIP

Sound design, step by step

  1. 01Watch the picture several times without sound. List the sounds it should have.
  2. 02Lay dialogue and narration first and lock their levels.
  3. 03Add music and match it to the cuts and beats.
  4. 04Sync effects to the action frame by frame.
  5. 05Lay ambience to create space. The quieter the scene, the more it needs ambience.
  6. 06Normalize overall loudness to the platform's standard.
  7. 07Check on three setups: earphones, speakers and a smartphone.
