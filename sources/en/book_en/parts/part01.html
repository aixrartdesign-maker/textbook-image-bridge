PART 01

Introduction

The first wall anyone who sets out to learn video hits is not technique but information overload. There are tens of thousands of videos on camera settings, editing programs change their interface every year, and since 2022 generative AI has joined in, so what you learned yesterday is outdated today. Many people wear out chasing tools.

Step back a little and the picture changes. When the Lumière brothers filmed a train arriving in 1895, the questions were already there: how much to put in the frame, how long to show it. The principles the Soviet filmmakers discovered experimenting with montage in the 1920s still drive the cut editing of short-form today. Tools change every 10 years; the language of video has stood almost still for over 100.

That is why this part starts with history. Know why 24 frames, why a 180-degree shutter, why faces grow as screens shrink, and your judgment holds when a new tool appears. This is not about memorizing a timeline. It is about understanding what constraints and compromises produced each standard we now take for granted.

Background 1

What does the moving image hold on to?

Video is a technique for recording light and an old desire to hold on to time. In "The Ontology of the Photographic Image" (1945), the French critic André Bazin traced the origin of the plastic arts to the Egyptian mummy: the desire to preserve the body's form against death, the "mummy complex." Photography is special because light, not a human hand, leaves the trace of the object directly, and cinema goes a step further and embalms time itself.

Around the same time, Walter Benjamin asked an entirely different question in "The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction" (first draft 1935). A painting has a uniqueness that exists only here and now, its "aura." But photography and film reproduce the same image endlessly, and audiences meet it in theaters and living rooms. Benjamin saw this change as toppling the authority of art while opening a new mode of perception to the masses. A short video reaching millions of phones at once sits at the far edge of the world he foresaw.

So how do still photographs appear to move? For a long time textbooks explained it with "persistence of vision": the image lingering on the retina overlaps the next one. But since Max Wertheimer's apparent-motion experiments of 1912, researchers understand motion perception as the brain's interpretation rather than the retina's. Two lights flashing alternately at the right interval feel like one light moving. Video is ultimately an illusion completed inside the viewer's brain, and that fact is the starting point for understanding editing and animation.

In the two volumes of Cinema in the 1980s, the philosopher Gilles Deleuze divided the film image into the "movement-image" and the "time-image." In classical cinema time flows along a chain of action and reaction. In postwar European cinema, scenes where characters cannot act and merely look, and time that drifts without purpose, began to fill the screen. An ad that tries to provoke a response within 3 seconds speaks the language of the movement-image; a brand film that leaves room with long takes borrows the sense of the time-image.

Every technique in this book returns to three questions. What to hold on to, whom to deliver it to, and what to let complete itself in the viewer's mind. The video maker is a technician of light and a designer of the audience's perception and memory.

Further reading — André Bazin, "The Ontology of the Photographic Image," What Is Cinema? · Walter Benjamin, "The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction" · Gilles Deleuze, Cinema 1: The Movement-Image, Cinema 2: The Time-Image

Defining video and the principles of perception

Nothing actually moves. Movement is made not on the screen but in human perception.

Term

Apparent motion — stimuli that are in fact still appear to move through the brain's interpretation.

Video is a medium that strings still pictures together fast enough to be perceived as motion. The fact that video production is ultimately the design of the viewer's cognition starts here.

Afterimages and the phi phenomenon

The old explanationPersistence of vision

  • The retinal image lingers about 0.05 s after the stimulus is gone
  • Cannot explain the sense of smooth motion

1912 · WertheimerPhi phenomenon

  • The brain fills the gap between two stimuli in different positions with movement
  • Today's main explanation of motion perception

IntegrationBeta movement

  • Successive still images in different positions are integrated as one moving object

About 50HzFlicker fusion threshold

  • The rate at which flicker begins to feel like steady light
  • Why early projectors flashed each frame two or three times
16fpsMotion is perceived but judder remains
24fpsThe compromise between smooth motion and film consumption
0.05sHow long an afterimage lingers on the retina
TIP

The cinematic feel of 24fps is not a physical necessity but a convention learned over 100 years. Use frame rate as an emotional tool.

12.6

From analog video to digital

Recording made editing possible, and nonlinear editing changed the very way we think about editing.

  • 1956 Broadcast videotape recorders become practical. TV is no longer live-only
  • 1970s–80s Home video format war and the spread of camcorders. Individuals shoot and keep their own footage
  • 1989 Nonlinear editing systems go commercial. Instant access to any point
  • 1995 The DV format. Digital quality on cheap tape; independent production explodes
  • 1999–2003 Desktop editing software spreads. The personal computer becomes the edit suite
  • 2008 DSLRs that shoot video arrive. Large-sensor shallow depth of field at low cost — the DSLR revolution
  • 2010s Mirrorless · cinema cameras, 4K, log recording and gimbals become standard tools

Linear editing mindset

  • Work only in sequence
  • Inserting mid-sequence is very hard
  • One mistake means starting over
  • Must be fully planned in advance
  • The editor's planning skill is key

Nonlinear editing mindset

  • Instant access to any point
  • Endless trial and error
  • Version control is the new challenge
  • Exploration over planning
  • The editor's selection skill is key
Note

Being able to revise endlessly makes you put off decisions.

The spread of nonlinear editing has clear side effects too. This is exactly why the habit of drawing a storyboard before shooting still matters.

12.10

Working principles from history

Six working principles drawn from history. What 130 years of history left behind is not a timeline but six sentences you can put straight into a proposal today.

  1. 01The medium makes the form. Screen size, viewing distance, ambient noise and controls decide a piece's length and shot sizes. Start planning without choosing a platform and you will end up remaking it.
  2. 02Meaning lies between cuts. A principle unchanged since Kuleshov. Before gathering good material, decide the order you will join it in.
  3. 03New technology is a constraint first. A new tool narrows expression at first. Use it before others do, but put it in the final piece only when you can absorb its limits into your direction.
  4. 04Standards are conventions. 24fps, 16:9 and the 180-degree shutter are agreements, not laws of physics. Know the reasons and you can break them on purpose.
  5. 05Smaller screen, bigger everything. As the screen shrinks, subjects get bigger, cuts get shorter and captions grow. The core rule for mobile.
  6. 06Distribution changes creation. Since YouTube removed the distribution barrier, planning starts not from the idea but from discoverability. That is why titles and thumbnails became part of directing.
TIP

12.12

The birth and principles of photography

A story from the dark room to the smartphone. Photography was born before video, and nearly every video technology stands on photographic technology.

Term

Camera obscura — Latin for 'dark room'. Light entering through a small hole projects the outside scene upside down on the opposite wall.

Photography began with attempts to fix with chemicals this image, which painters had used since the Renaissance as a drawing aid.

The five elements of a camera

01Dark boxThe body that lets in only the light you want. The word comes from the Latin for room (camera)
02LensGathers more light to a point than a pinhole. Focal length sets the angle of view
03ApertureSize of the opening light passes through. Changes brightness and depth of field together
04ShutterHow long light comes in. Freezes motion or lets it flow
05Recording surfaceOn film, silver halide grains turn black to leave an image; on a sensor, pixels turn light into charge, stored as numbers

How it developed

  • 1826–27 Niépce The oldest surviving photograph, View from the Window at Le Gras. An exposure of several hours
  • 1839 Daguerre The daguerreotype. Very sharp on a silver plate, but a single image that cannot be copied
  • 1841 Talbot The calotype. Many positives from one paper negative — the start of the negative · positive system of copying
  • 1851 Archer Wet plate (collodion). The sharpness of glass negatives with exposures of seconds. Portrait studios go mainstream
  • 1888 Eastman Roll-film box camera. "You press the button, we do the rest" — shooting separated from developing
  • 1907 Autochrome · 1935 Kodachrome The first commercial color photography process, and color film with multilayer emulsion
  • 1925 35mm compact camera Movie film used for stills. Street photography and photojournalism; today's full-frame sensor standard
  • 1948 Instant photography A photo in your hand within minutes. The start of seeing results right away
  • 1975 Digital prototype Kodak's Steven Sasson records about 0.01 megapixels onto cassette tape
  • 2000s onward Camera phones and smartphones put shooting · retouching · sharing in one device. Computational photography that merges many frames becomes standard

Link to video

Motion picture film is the technique of exposing photographic film at 24 frames in 1 second. Aperture, shutter and sensitivity carried straight over into video (3.5).

In photography's history, as exposure times shortened, what could be photographed widened to things in motion. Muybridge's 1878 sequence of a running horse is the bridge between photography and cinema.

12.13

From darkroom to print

From silver gelatin prints to CMYK printing. Before digital, a chemical process stood between shooting and seeing. Screens add light; print subtracts it.

Term

Negative — film whose exposed areas turn black, reversing light and dark. Projected onto photo paper again, it becomes a positive with the tones restored.

The five steps of film developing

01DevelopTurns exposed silver grains into black metallic silver. Temperature and time decide the result
02StopA weak acid bath halts development
03FixDissolves unexposed silver so it no longer reacts
04Wash and dryWash out chemicals thoroughly and dry somewhere dust-free. Leftover chemicals cause discoloration years later

Printing — editing under the enlarger

SelectingContact print

  • Negatives laid on photo paper to see every frame on one sheet
  • Choosing which frames to use is the origin of editing

EnlargingEnlarger

  • A lens projects the negative large, printed at the size you want
  • Exposure time is brightness

RefiningDodging and burning

  • Hands or card hold back or add light in places
  • Where Photoshop's dodge · burn tools got their names

WorkspaceRed safelight

  • Black-and-white paper barely reacts to red light
  • Lets you see what you are doing

How color is printed

Additive mixingRGB

  • The three primaries of light. Adding brightens; all combined make white
  • Monitors · smartphones · projectors

Subtractive mixingCMY + K

  • Ink primaries cyan · magenta · yellow. Layering darkens
  • Overlapping CMY gives a muddy brown, so a separate black (K, key) ink is used

GradationHalftone dots

  • Builds tones from dot size and spacing instead of ink density
  • Each plate set at a different angle to avoid moiré

ProofingColor conversion

  • Printing an RGB image as is loses saturation
  • Convert to CMYK, then check on a proof

One-page summary

12.1 Principles of perception

Motion is made not on the screen but in the viewer's perception. Making video is designing cognition.

12.4 · 12.10 Standards are conventions

24 frames, 16:9 and the 180-degree shutter are not laws of physics but the result of technical compromise and convention. Know the reasons and you can break them on purpose.

12.2 · 12.14 Viewing conditions and form

Just as the Kinetoscope created short attractions, the vertical screen created short-form.

12.3 · 12.11 Meaning between cuts

Meaning arises not within one cut but between two. A principle unchanged since Kuleshov.

12.4 · 12.8 New technology is a constraint first

New technology always arrives first as a constraint and only later becomes a means of expression. Generative AI is on the same path.

12.5 · 12.7 Smaller screen, bigger everything

As the screen shrinks, subjects get bigger, cuts get shorter and captions grow.

12.6 Nonlinear editing

Being able to revise endlessly makes you put off decisions. That is why a storyboard before shooting still matters.

12.12 · 12.13 From photography to print

A camera works on just five principles: box · lens · aperture · shutter · recording surface. Screens add light; print subtracts it.

Questions to consider

Explain why 24 frames became the standard, separating technical constraint from convention.
The technical constraint is the compromise between film consumption and continuity of motion; the convention is the 'cinematic feel' learned over the 100 years since.
Consider what way of viewing the video you are making now assumes, and how that assumption is changing its form.
Write on the first line of your proposal what device it will be watched on, where, and by how many people together, and the answers on length · shot size · captions follow.

Field tips

  • In meetings, don't describe a reference in words; send the link with the time range. Just writing "from 3:45 to 4:10" cuts meeting time in half.
  • When learning a new tool, first find out not its feature list but the existing process it replaces. Know its place and you learn faster.
  • If you don't know an industry term, ask on the spot. The term you pretended to know causes the biggest accidents.
