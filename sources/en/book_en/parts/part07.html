PART 07

Introduction

When the screen is not a rectangle, when the audience walks instead of sitting, the rules of planning change at the root. 360-degree video has no frame, an exhibition has no pause button, and an outdoor ad has no sound. Carry conventional film grammar into these conditions unchanged, and you will fail.

Still, the principles carry over. Without a frame you must still guide the eye; with a walking audience you must still design rhythm; without sound you still need dynamics. Only the means change: light and sound draw the eye instead of framing, the visitor's path makes rhythm instead of cuts, and contrast makes dynamics instead of music.

Specs are a search away, but little has been written on how a person in a cinema seat and a person scrolling on the subway take in the same video differently. Knowing that difference is a planner's real edge.

Background 11

A history of screens and the audience's body

Where audiences watch has moved from the theater seat to the living-room sofa, to the screen in the palm, and into devices worn on the head. Each time the screen changed, so did the grammar of the moving image.

In the late 18th century the British painter Robert Barker introduced the "panorama," wrapping the entire wall of a round building in a landscape painting. Viewers stood at the center and took in cities and battlefields spread across 360 degrees. The desire to step into a picture is older than film; today's 360 video and immersive exhibitions are its latest form.

As television entered homes, the film industry widened the screen to stress an experience only theaters could give. The result was Cinerama and CinemaScope in the 1950s, and IMAX, first shown at the 1970 Osaka Expo. With wider screens, directors developed compositions that set a character to one side and let landscape and empty space carry the emotion.

Change also ran the other way. Morton Heilig's 1962 "Sensorama" added vibration, wind, and smell to film, and in 1968 Ivan Sutherland showed an early head-mounted display. Here the audience is no longer looking at a screen but standing inside it. When the medium changes, the audience's body changes; when the body changes, the grammar changes.

The smartphone made the screen small again, and vertical. On a screen held in one hand, the human face becomes the key subject, and captions and interface become part of the image. As Marshall McLuhan said in Understanding Media (1964), "the medium is the message": the same content is received differently depending on the medium that carries it.

The body responds differently to different screen sizes. In a theater the screen fills most of the field of view, so wide landscapes and a slow pace work; a vertical screen in the hand fills only part of it, and the viewer can flick to the next video at any moment. So if you change only the aspect ratio, the rhythm collapses. Cut length, information density, even the size of the figures must be redesigned.

Sound conditions differ too. A theater assumes multichannel sound and low noise; a phone means a small speaker or earphones, and in public people watch muted. Outdoor LED and in-store signage should be assumed to have no sound at all. That is why captions and graphics become the main carriers. Behind every number in a spec table, picture the audience's posture and surroundings.

Further reading — Marshall McLuhan, Understanding Media · Lev Manovich, The Language of New Media · Anne Friedberg, The Virtual Window

Horizontal and vertical

Not a different medium but a different language. Vertical is not cropped horizontal. Long shots don't survive, and captions become a major part of the frame.

Grammar of horizontal 16:9

  • Wide sides, good for relationships and setting
  • Long and medium shots carry ample information
  • Bottom captions need little eye travel
  • Suits two-person dialogue, landscape, architecture
  • Default for TV, cinema, long-form YouTube

Grammar of vertical 9:16

  • Tall frame suits full-body shots and close-ups
  • Long shots turn to mush; rarely used
  • Subject on the central vertical axis
  • Captions become a major part of the frame
  • Default for short-form, stories, live commerce
TIP

The surest way is to shoot the same scene twice, horizontal and vertical. It takes twice as long, but the results are best.

Making a vertical version from horizontal footage

  1. 01Shoot in 4K or higher. A vertical crop from FHD horizontal lacks resolution.
  2. 02Show a 9:16 guide in the viewfinder and keep the main subject centered.
  3. 03Wide left-to-right compositions don't survive in vertical. If a vertical version is planned, change the composition at the shoot.
  4. 04Place captions separately for the horizontal and vertical versions. Carry positions over as is, and the UI will cover them.
  5. 05Shooting the same scene twice, horizontal and vertical, is the surest way.

Background 12

How immersion is made

Before working with immersive content, we should be clear about what it means for an audience to feel "inside" it. Immersion is a matter of conditions, not image quality.

The history of immersive technology begins with attempts to enlarge the screen. In the 1950s Cinerama filled a curved screen with three projectors to cover even peripheral vision, followed by CinemaScope and 70mm film. Heilig's 1962 Sensorama added vibration, wind, and even smell, and Sutherland's 1968 head-mounted display was so heavy it had to hang from the ceiling, earning the nickname "the Sword of Damocles." The goal was always the same: to pull the audience's vision and senses as far toward the screen as possible.

Researchers split this experience into two concepts. Immersion is the objective condition that equipment and content provide: field of view, response speed to head movement, directionality of sound. Presence is the subjective state the user actually feels, the sense that "I am here." With the same equipment, presence varies greatly with content design. That is why an expensive headset can still deliver a flat experience.

The most common presence-breakers are latency and mismatch. When the image lags a turn of the head, the brain detects a gap between the motion the eyes see and the motion the vestibular system feels. This is the main cause of cybersickness. So VR production keeps frame rates high, avoids camera moves the user did not make, and uses mitigations such as vignetting that masks the edges of the view. Stability comes before image quality.

The premises of directing change too. In 360 video and VR the audience can look anywhere, so the director has only means to guide, not to force. The direction a sound comes from, differences in brightness, a character's gaze and movement, and the layout of the space turn the viewer's head. To keep viewers from missing a key event, plant cues before it happens.

In AR and projection mapping the image sits on real space, so actual light levels, surface materials, and where the audience stands decide the result. A dark wall in an indoor gallery and a building facade at midday are entirely different media. The quality of immersive content is decided not by the final file but by the place where it will be shown.

Further reading — Mel Slater, "A Note on Presence Terminology" · Jaron Lanier, Dawn of the New Everything · Jason Jerald, The VR Book

Four principles for planning AR ads

TIP

Think of captions for glasses as one line at a time, a few words per line. Deliver to the ear first, not the eye, and use text as support.

Three things change for makers. First, text appears small at the edge of vision, not in the middle of a screen. Unless it is short, large, and high-contrast, it won't be read.

Second, voice comes first. Glasses are a hands-free medium, so a flow of asking and answering replaces touch. Third, 1st-person video becomes the default format. Every head movement is captured, and aspect ratios vary by device, so you can't assume horizontal 16:9.

"Beyond the lens," as film first drew it

The Terminator's red vision HUD identified objects in view and overlaid data; Minority Report showed a gesture UI that drags screens around in midair; and Iron Man's helmet HUD planted the idea of a 1st-person interface where information floats with your gaze.

The "short text at the edge of vision," "gesture control," and "voice that describes what you see" of today's AR glasses are all grammar that film drew first. Audiences have seen those screens dozens of times, so AR content reads without explanation.

Dwell-time math

Combine the length of 1 session with simultaneous capacity to get hourly throughput. A 90-second experience for 4 people at once tops out at 160 people per hour in theory; in real operation, add changeover and briefing time and plan on about 70% of that figure.

MR in museums

Museums are where MR is most convincing, because the constraint that originals cannot be touched or moved becomes MR's very strength.

The National Museum of Korea runs a digital immersive gallery within its permanent exhibition, showing content such as a VR Goguryeo mural tomb, large panoramic films, and a digital Gwanggaeto Stele, and major national museums abroad also run artifact-restoration AR and MR tours. Their setups mostly come down to three elements.

  • Restoration holograms — the missing shards of broken pottery, or a worn Buddha statue's original colors, laid over the real object. Keep some transparency so the line between original and restoration shows.
  • Docent avatars — read the visitor's gaze and position to explain the artifact they are looking at. Keep the talk under 30 seconds per artifact; the visitor chooses whether to hear more.
  • Hand-tracking control — pick up, turn, and enlarge artifacts by hand, without controllers. Place objects within arm's reach (about 0.5–0.7 m) and have them return to place when handling ends.
Note

In museum MR, hygiene and turnover are half the plan. Put headset sanitizing time between users and briefing time into the dwell-time chart.

Projection mapping

Build the content on an unfolded template of the target. It works best when the edges of real structures, such as windows and columns, become the structure of the content.

PrincipleMeasure → match → correct → calibrate

  • Measure the target's 3D form and rebuild it as a virtual model
  • Match the projector position to the virtual camera
  • Correct distortion per surface, render video
  • Fine-tune with on-site calibration after installation

Planning factorsSurface · ambient light · distance · blending

  • Dark, rough surfaces lose a lot of brightness
  • Night or blackout conditions required
  • Work out throw distance and brightness (ANSI lumens)
  • With multiple projectors, leave blend zones
Term

TIP

Visitors' phones are the best distribution network. Design photo-zone lighting and backdrops for shooting and sharing.

  1. 01Within 3 seconds of entering, visitors should grasp what the place is for.
  2. 02Put a waiting area and a preview before each experience zone. Waiting time is content too.
  3. 03Choose clearly between a one-way route and free roaming. Mix them and you get bottlenecks and backflow.
  4. 04Place the most striking scene just before the exit to shape the last memory.
  5. 05Design photo-zone lighting and backdrops for shooting and sharing.

Order of work when one piece spans several media

Build first for the most constrained medium. If you must do both short-form and outdoor, lock a single message for outdoor, then expand into short-form. Do it the other way and the information overflows until nothing reads outdoors.

New displays and spatial media

Since the mid-2020s, the surfaces that carry video have multiplied fast. Planners must know their minimum specs to hold a conversation with vendors.

Large TVs and the living room

NOTE

If you make an HDR master, always make an SDR version with it.

  • 4K is now standard, and 8K panels have reached budget models. But 8K content is scarce, so upscaled playback is the norm.
  • As HDR panels spread, the range of brightness has widened.
  • As screens grow, viewing distance is shrinking to 1.5–2 times the diagonal. That is why broadcast grammar, once close-up driven, is widening again.
  • Smart TV apps have brought YouTube and streaming into the living room. Also consider vertical short-form playing on a large horizontal screen.

Headsets and spatial video

Apple brought spatial video to the public with Vision Pro, Meta spread passthrough mixed reality with its Quest line, and Google is betting on an Android-based XR platform and lightweight AR glasses. They share one thing: the screen breaks out of the rectangular frame and expands to the user's entire field of view.

Spatial videoStereo capture and long takes

  • Two lenses record at once, spaced about like human eyes. A wider baseline adds depth but strains the eyes up close
  • Frequent cuts are uncomfortable, as the brain needs time to rebuild depth. Long takes are the norm
  • Place captions as objects with a depth value. If the depth is off, focus wavers

AR glasses · passthroughBlack and brand colors

  • Optical see-through can't show black. Dark tones simply turn transparent
  • Passthrough replays reality shot by cameras, so color and exposure follow the device. Don't count on accurate brand colors

PART 07Media specs and immersive content
6.14

Dimensions of form

Point · line · plane · volume · time. Immersive content goes beyond the screen and treats the whole space as material for form. From point to time, motion adds one dimension at a time.

Reference photos 6.14A · 6.14BGenerated images — directing reference
Counting dimensions

The point starts at 0D, so volume is 3D and adding time makes 4D. Remember that point, line, plane, volume, time each add one motion, in that order, and you won't get confused.

In geometry, a point has position but no size and is 0D; a line, the path of a moving point, is 1D; a plane, the path of a moving line, is 2D; and a volume, the path of a moving plane, is 3D. Add time and it becomes 4D, and when the viewer walks inside, movement through place is added.

How immersive content uses each dimension

0PointA single light in the dark, a single particle. The strongest device for drawing the eye to one spot
1LineLight trails, lasers, LED lines. They show direction and speed and guide movement
2PlaneScreens, walls, floors, translucent scrims. Projection mapping turns a building's planes into screens
3VolumeVolumetric lighting, hologram effects, anamorphic illusion. Make volume appear on a plane or create it in the air
4TimeChange and motion. The same space becomes an entirely different scene over time
+Moving through placeThe scene changes as visitors walk. Film moves place with cuts; in immersive space, the visitor's body moves place
The more video moves from the flat plane into space, the more the planner designs the viewer's position instead of the camera's.6.14 Video is space-time in motion

Video records 3D space on a 2D screen and adds time to set it in motion. Editing cuts and joins that space-time (1.20); immersive content brings the viewer's body inside it.

Artists and theory to read alongside

  • Wassily Kandinsky, Point and Line to Plane (1926) — sets out the tension among point · line · plane as the basic grammar of form.
  • Paul Klee, Pedagogical Sketchbook (1925) — describes the line as a point taking a walk: the idea that motion makes dimension.
  • Kazimir Malevich, Piet Mondrian — Black Square (1915) and horizontal · vertical divisions of the plane: a picture of plane and color alone.
  • Picasso and Braque's Cubism — layering many viewpoints in one picture to bring volume onto the plane.
  • Marcel Duchamp, Étienne-Jules Marey — Nude Descending a Staircase, No. 2 (1912) and chronophotography put time and motion in one frame.
  • Nam June Paik — video art that used TV sets as sculptural material, turning the screen into an object in space.
  • Yayoi Kusama, James Turrell, teamLab — works of dots, light and space that viewers step into. The first references for planning immersive exhibitions.

A sentence to use on set

In an immersive content proposal, write first where visitors stand, where they walk and what they pass, before what you will show.

One-page summary

6.1 Production specs by medium

A project started without a set platform always gets remade. Recheck the official guides before you begin.

6.2 Horizontal and vertical

Vertical is not a different medium but a different language. Long shots don't survive.

6.3 Safe areas

Check with a real upload, not the editing software's guides.

6.5 360 and VR

If the viewer never looked behind them, the plan failed. Preventing motion sickness comes before image quality.

6.7 XR exhibition halls

Divide 3600 seconds by the experience length, multiply by simultaneous capacity, and take 70% of that as the realistic figure.

6.9 Outdoor ads

Outdoor ads are silent by default. A plan that relies on sound has failed at the choice of medium.

6.10 LED signage

Terms like pixel pitch and nits make quotes more accurate. On transparent LED, black is transparent.

6.11 Viewing environments

When spanning several media, build first for the most constrained one. Expanding is easy; shrinking is hard.

In a proposal, write where visitors stand and where they walk before what you will show.
