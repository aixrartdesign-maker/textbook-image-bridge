PART 03

Introduction

A camera is a device that records light. Grasp this one sentence and the order of operation is the same on any model. How much light to let in, for how long, and how sensitively to receive it. Every other function supports these three.

The most common beginner mistake is leaving it all to auto. Auto exposure shifts brightness every time a cloud passes, and auto white balance gives each cut a different color. Footage is material that must cut together, so consistency comes before the polish of any single cut.

This part is full of numbers, but you don't need to memorize them. What you need is a feel for what you gain and lose when you turn each dial. Open the aperture and the background blurs, but focus misses rise; raise the sensitivity and the image brightens, but noise grows. Every choice has a price, and the DP is the one who knows the price and pays it.

Background 4

Lens and eye — machine vision and human vision

The camera is often likened to the eye, but the two do not see the world the same way. The human eye sees sharply only in a tiny area called the fovea; the eyes dart about, and the brain stitches the fragments into one scene. That is why we feel we can see a bright window and a dark room equally well at once.

A camera sensor records the whole frame under the same conditions in one instant, so the view outside blows out to white or the interior sinks into black. We study exposure and dynamic range because the maker must now decide what the eye used to do automatically.

Focal length is also commonly misunderstood. Strictly speaking, what changes perspective is not focal length but the distance between camera and subject. To fill the frame with a face on a wide lens you must get close, and that distance makes the nose look big. To get the same size on a telephoto you must step far back, and that distance makes the background look close. Perspective is changed by the camera's position, not the lens.

'Normal lens' is also closer to a convention. A full-frame sensor's diagonal is about 43mm, so focal lengths near it are taken as closest to natural perspective, and by custom 50mm is called normal. What matters more than the number is whether the audience takes the scene as 'seeing it up close' or as 'peeking from afar'.

The f-number is the ratio of focal length to the diameter of the aperture opening. f/2 has a larger opening than f/4 and lets in four times the light, and the larger the opening, the shallower the depth of field. Depth of field also varies with focal length, subject distance and sensor size, so for a shallow look you can also move closer to the subject or put the background farther away. Technical terms form one connected system.

In the end, a camera is not a device that imitates the eye but one that shows the world in ways the eye cannot. It freezes 0.1 seconds, stretches 1 second into 10, and sees from impossible heights and distances. The numbers in this chapter are the language for using that new vision as you intend.

Further reading — Blain Brown, Cinematography: Theory and Practice · Gustavo Mercado, The Filmmaker's Eye

Sensor size and angle of view

The smaller the sensor, the narrower the same lens sees; the larger the sensor, the shallower the depth of field. The crop factor puts this difference into a number.

Crop factor

A multiplier showing how much narrower a lens of the same focal length sees than on full frame.

A 50mm on APS-C gives the angle of view of a 75mm on full frame. To fit a full body into a tight space, the smaller the sensor, the wider the lens you need.

The larger the sensor, the shallower the depth of field. A large sensor helps when you want a blurred background, but holding focus gets harder too. When everything must be sharp, as in product ads, a small sensor is actually easier.

3.5

The exposure triangle

There are only three ways to set image brightness, and each has its own price. The decisive difference from photography is that in video you can't use the shutter freely.

Aperture (F-number)The dial that sets the look

  • Lower number: brighter, blurrier background
  • F1.4, 2, 2.8, 4, 5.6, 8 — each step halves the light
  • Most direct expression of intent

Shutter speedFixed in video

  • Time spent recording one frame
  • Tied to the 180-degree shutter rule; never use it to adjust exposure

ISOBase ISO first

  • Raising it brightens but adds noise
  • Each model has a base ISO with the least noise
  • Many models have dual base ISO

ND filterThe fourth dial

  • Optical filter that only cuts light
  • Essential for shooting wide open in daylight
ND83 stopsOvercast days, indoors by a window
ND646 stopsWide open on sunny days
ND100010 stopsLong-exposure effects in daylight
TIP

Follow this order and your judgment holds on any camera.

Slow-motion formula

Shooting fps divided by timeline fps is the slow-down factor. 120fps footage on a 24fps timeline plays 5× slow. But 120fps needs about 5× the light of 24fps, so trying it without a lighting plan ends in noise.

Note

To keep the 180-degree rule outdoors in daylight, you need ND filters. Stop down instead and you lose the background blur you intended.

Rules on set

  • Auto white balance shifts color from cut to cut, so the shots won't cut together. Always lock it manually.
  • On mixed-light locations, gel the windows or swap the interior lights so every source is one color.
  • Setting a custom white balance off a gray or white card makes color correction in post easier.
  • Decide deliberate color shifts on set, not in post. Even in log, set a reference before you shoot.

Codecs · bit depth · log

Choose a different codec for each stage; shoot 10-bit if you'll grade and 4:2:2 or better if you'll composite. Log buys latitude in post and pays for it in exposure margin.

CodecCharacterFile sizeBest stageRemarks
H.264Compressed for deliveryVery smallFinal delivery, web uploadPoor as an edit source
H.265 (HEVC)Efficient compressionSmall4K camera originalsUse proxies to edit
ProRes 422 HQEdit-friendlyLargeStandard for edit and compDe facto industry standard
ProRes 4444Supports alpha channelVery largeMotion graphics intermediatesAdvised for AE renders
DNxHRProRes alternativeLargeWindows editingAvid-compatible
RAWRaw sensor dataHugeTop-tier adsNeeds separate processing

Bit depth10-bit is the baseline

  • 8-bit: 256 levels per channel, about 16.7 million colors
  • 10-bit: 1024 levels per channel, over 1 billion colors
  • 8-bit shows banding in skies and gradients
  • The heavier the grade, the more you need 10-bit
  • Shoot 10-bit even for student projects

Chroma subsampling4:2:2 or better for chroma key

  • 4:4:4 no color loss, ideal for compositing
  • 4:2:2 half the color data, ad standard
  • 4:2:0 1/4 of the color data, consumer codecs
  • Keying 4:2:0 inevitably leaves jagged edges
Note

Log is not recommended for chroma key shoots. Its low contrast drains saturation from the green, and the key edges fall apart.

Log recording

Log compresses the wide brightness range the sensor captures. The image looks washed out and flat, but highlight and shadow detail survive, leaving lots of room in post. It is vulnerable to underexposure, though, so shooting slightly bright and pulling it down in post reduces noise.

Put a look LUT on the set monitor so people can picture the result, but always record the original log.

3.12

Mirrorless setup in practice

Menu names change with every generation, but the order of operations stays the same. Drill the sequence below and you can set up any model in under 5 minutes.

TIP

Check resolution, frame rate, shutter, picture profile, audio levels, and focus mode, in that order.

  1. 01Switch to movie manual mode. In auto, exposure shifts from cut to cut and won't match in the edit.
  2. 02Set the recording format and frame rate first. Choose 4K 24p or 4K 60p to suit the final use.
  3. 03Set the picture profile. Log if you'll grade in post, standard if you'll deliver straight away.
  4. 04Lock the shutter at double the frame rate. At 24p, 1/48 or 1/50.
  5. 05Lock ISO at base ISO. Log profiles often have a higher base ISO.
  6. 06Set white balance manually or measure a custom value.
  7. 07Decide depth of field with the aperture, and add an ND filter if it's too bright.
  8. 08Focus depends on the situation. Face-detection AF for a lone person; manual focus for products and compositing plates.
  9. 09Check with zebras or the histogram that highlights aren't clipping.
  10. 10Set audio levels manually and monitor on headphones.
  11. 11Check card space and battery level, then shoot a test and play it back.

Shooting with a smartphone

The smartphone is the most widely used camera. Know its limits and it can hold its own in commercial work. The key is not leaving it on auto.

Auto features you must turn off

FeatureProblem on autoManual fix
Auto exposureBrightness swings whenever a cloud passesExposure lock or pro mode
AutofocusFocus hunts mid-shotFocus lock or manual focus
Auto white balanceColor varies by cut; can't be edited togetherSet color temperature manually
Auto HDRFlattens texture, less room in postUse only when needed
Excessive stabilizationJello effect: wobbling edgesTurn off on a gimbal
TIP

Long-press to lock exposure and focus (AE/AF lock).

Seven ways to raise quality

  1. 01Stabilize. A tripod or mount alone gets you more than halfway there.
  2. 02Get close. Digital zoom throws away quality. Zoom with your feet.
  3. 03Find the light. Stand the person at a window, facing it, and you need no lights.
  4. 04Wipe the lens. Pocket fingerprints lower contrast across the whole frame.
  5. 05Record sound separately. Use an external mic or a separate recorder.
  6. 06Shoot log or a flat profile. Recent models support log.
  7. 07Shoot 4K to leave room for both vertical and horizontal versions.

When a smartphone is actually better

Tight spaces, first-person views, macro, everyday-life scenes, and situations where you mustn't look like you're filming. Point a big camera at people and they freeze, but they react naturally to a phone. Choosing a tool isn't only about image quality.

Note

Drone laws and approval procedures change often. When planning, check the latest rules with the relevant authority yourself and build the approval lead time into the schedule.

Drone shooting in practice

  1. 01Check where you may fly and whether approval is needed. Airspace over airports, military sites, and crowded areas is restricted.
  2. 02Check pilot certification and drone registration requirements. They vary with the drone's weight.
  3. 03Check the weather. Wind speed, rain, and sunrise and sunset times decide whether you can shoot.
  4. 04At least 3 sets of batteries. Each set gives about 15 minutes of real shooting.
  5. 05Before takeoff, check the compass and GPS signal and set the return-to-home altitude.
  6. 06Do one simple move at a time. Pushing forward, rising, and rotating at once makes the image dizzying.
  7. 07The most useful shots are a rising opener and a pull-back-and-rise ending. Get these two first.
  8. 08Post one safety spotter to watch for people and obstacles nearby.

One-page summary

3.2 Choosing a camera

If it moves, stabilization; if it's dark, a big sensor; if you'll crop in, high resolution.

3.3 Sensor and angle of view

50mm on APS-C is 75mm on full frame. The bigger the sensor, the shallower the depth of field.

3.4 The psychology of lenses

Perspective is changed by the camera's position, not the lens.

3.5 The exposure triangle

Lock the shutter, lock ISO at base, shape with the aperture, and handle leftover brightness with ND and lighting.

3.7 Resolution

Shoot 4K even if the final output is FHD. Spare pixels are editing freedom.

3.8 Shutter angle

A 180° shutter is double the frame rate: 1/120 at 60p, 1/240 at 120p. High frame rates demand proportionally more light.

3.9 White balance

Auto exposure and auto WB make cuts impossible to join. Always lock them manually.

3.10 Codecs and log

Chroma key edges hold up at 4:2:2 or better. This is the one shoot where log isn't advised.

3.11 Camera support

Tripod → slider → gimbal. A lit tripod beats an unlit gimbal.

3.13 Smartphones

Stabilize, get close, find the light, wipe the lens, record sound separately.

Questions to consider

Why shouldn't the shutter be used to control exposure in video?
The shutter is bound to the 180-degree rule and sets the texture of motion blur, so treat it as a fixed value.
What is the difference between ND8 and ND64 in stops?
ND8 cuts 3 stops, ND64 6 stops, and ND1000 10 stops.
Why is a 4:2:0 codec bad for chroma key?
With only 1/4 of the color data, keyed edges come out jagged.
Why shoot log slightly bright?
Log is vulnerable to underexposure, so shooting bright and pulling down in post reduces noise.
When must you shoot 4K for an FHD final output, and when not?
If you expect reframing, stabilization, or a vertical version, 4K is a must. Work out for yourself the cases where it isn't.

Field tips

  • The day before the shoot, count cards and batteries and photograph them. It's your evidence when someone on set says something is missing.
  • Check focus with magnification before the director checks the frame. A soft shot can only be fixed by a reshoot.
  • When renting gear, photograph its condition on return. 90% of damage disputes end with that one photo.
