Background 23

The man who turned television around

In 1963, "Exposition of Music — Electronic Television" opened in a small gallery in Wuppertal, Germany. Thirteen televisions stood in the room. Some had distorted pictures; on others the image changed when visitors pressed a foot pedal or made a sound into a microphone. It was the first solo show of Nam June Paik, born in Seoul in 1932, and is now called the starting point of video art.

Television then was a device for receiving at home whatever the broadcaster sent. Paik set out to reverse that one-way flow. He let viewers intervene in the picture, and later built his own video synthesizer for shaping the video signal by hand. He turned a medium of receiving into a medium of making.

He joined Fluxus in 1962, part of a movement erasing the line between everyday life and art, and after meeting John Cage in 1958 he brought an Eastern sense of time into his work. In "TV Buddha" (1974), a Buddha statue sits before a television, gazing at its own image relayed by a camera. Meditation and closed circuit, the cyclical time of the East and the electronics of the West meet on one screen.

His vision reached beyond the work, too. In a 1974 proposal to the Rockefeller Foundation he first used the term 'electronic superhighway,' 20 years before the internet became widely known. On January 1, 1984, he aired "Good Morning Mr. Orwell," a live broadcast linking New York, Paris and Seoul by satellite. Where Orwell had pictured 1984 as the year of surveillance, Paik set out that same year to show the other side: technology could also connect people.

"The More, the Better," built at the MMCA Gwacheon for the 1988 Seoul Olympics, is a tower of 1,003 televisions stacked 18.5 meters high. It went dark in 2018 and came back on in 2022 after 4 years and 7 months of conservation work. The restoration exposed media art's hardest problem. If its parts are discontinued, what does the work survive as? The conservation issues discussed later start here.

Further reading — Nam June Paik Art Center, The Return of Nam June Paik · MMCA, My Nam June Paik: Memory, Conservation, Expansion (2023) · Lev Manovich, The Language of New Media

13.1

What is media art?

Media art is less a genre than an attitude. It is the umbrella term for work that takes the new media of its time as material and explores how those media change people and the world. In the 1960s that was television, in the 1990s the internet, and now sensors, data and generative models.

Why there are so many names

Video artLate 1960s

  • Emerged as portable video gear spread
  • Twisting TV's language, or showing viewers themselves on closed circuit

Media artA broader name

  • Came into use as materials widened beyond video to computers, networks and sensors

New media artFrom the 1990s

  • Work premised on digital and the internet
  • Weakness: the 'new' grows old with time

Post-internet artAfter the internet became like air

  • A contested term with no agreed academic definition
Note

You don't need to memorize the terms. But borrow one to describe your work without knowing what it means, and reviewers will spot it at once.

13.2

How video and media art differ

What's different? If you've read this far, you already have most of the skills you need. Reading a frame, building light, joining cuts and handling sound all still apply. What changes is not the skills but the premises.

Two things are the same. Both are time-based media, so length · rhythm · repetition · stillness are means of expression, and the production process of shooting · lighting · editing · grading · sound is still needed. The bigger the screen, the more your fundamentals show.

Regular video

  • Viewer position — seated in front of the screen
  • Start and end — fixed
  • Aspect ratio — can change to suit the medium
  • Equipment — a means of showing the result
  • Completion — done on delivery
  • Repetition — assumes a single viewing

Media art

  • Viewer position — enters and moves through the space
  • Start and end — viewers come and go at any time
  • Aspect ratio — a condition of the work; changing it is damage
  • Equipment — often part of the work
  • Completion — done only after running the whole show
  • Repetition — assumes hundreds of loops a day
TIP

Carry over a four-part arc as is, and most viewers see only the 'turn' and leave. It must read within 30 seconds from wherever they start watching.

What students miss most often: the start and end

Loop designMake the end join the start

  • If the last frame resembles the first, the seam disappears

Entry pointsReadable wherever they come in

  • Repeat the key image periodically

LengthBetween 2 and 7 minutes

  • Any longer and far fewer people watch to the end

Why you must keep the aspect ratio

Installation guidelines from media art conservation bodies specify that a work's aspect ratio be preserved, and state that enlarging or cropping to fit a screen is not proper practice. In video production, recutting for vertical is routine; with an artwork, that act itself changes the work. Here you must let go of the habit of reframing every time you change media.

Make exhibition video on the premise that nobody watches from the start. Making it readable from any entry point is the design.13.2 How video and media art differ

13.4

Tool map

What do you make it with? An editing tool produces one fixed output, but media art tools usually make 'a program that keeps running in real time.' You design for operation, not completion.

Structure

Read interactive media art in three boxes: sensor input (camera · depth · mic · touch) → processing (TouchDesigner · Unity · p5.js) → output (projection · LED · sound). The tool map below follows the same order.

Tools that make real-time videoProcessing

  • TouchDesigner — Wire nodes together to make real-time video. Links sensors · sound · video · projection output in one window; the most widely used tool for exhibitions, free under a non-commercial license
  • Unreal Engine · Unity — Game engines. 3D space, VR · AR, virtual production. Camera and lighting concepts carry straight over
  • Processing · p5.js — Drawing with code. Generative basics; p5.js runs right in the browser, the lowest barrier to entry
  • Max/MSP · Pure Data — Sound and signal. For sound-led or sound-reactive work

Tools that take inputInput

  • Depth camera — A person's distance and pose. The basic input for motion-reactive work
  • LiDAR — Scans space as points. Position tracking in large spaces, turning space into data
  • Arduino · Raspberry Pi — Small computers that run sensors and motors. Read light · sound · distance · pressure and turn it into video
  • Webcam and video analysis — One camera for motion detection, contour extraction, face tracking. The most realistic option with no budget

Tools that send outputOutput

  • Resolume — Plays and mixes video live. The standard for concert and club visuals
  • MadMapper — Dedicated to projection mapping. Fits video onto surfaces that aren't flat
  • NDI · Syphon — Channels for passing video between programs in real time. Essential once you have several machines
TIP

Don't pick the tool first. Write in one sentence 'what happens when the viewer does what,' then choose, and your options shrink to two or three.

13.5

Making it work in the gallery

Working in the studio and working every day for a month in a gallery are entirely different problems. A media artwork's reputation usually turns not on polish but on 'was it ever off?'

Light and distance

1/4Brightness when screen area quadruples. Brightness is rated in ANSI lumens; the same output looks dimmer on a bigger screen
About 3mDistance needed for a 2-meter-wide image with a 1.5 throw-ratio lens. Measure the space first, then choose the lens
  • Ambient light — In a brightly lit gallery, black lifts to gray. If dark areas matter, make light control a condition of the show.
  • Surface — A white wall is safest, but texture costs resolution. A dark surface deepens blacks but darkens everything.
Note

Screen savers, sleep mode and automatic updates are the most common causes of reboots during a show. Turn them all off.

Keeping it running

  1. 01Auto-start — When the power comes on, the program should launch itself with nobody there. OS auto-login and startup registration are the basics.
  2. 02No power saving — Turn off screen savers, sleep mode and automatic updates.
  3. 03Scheduled restart — Have it restart once every day before opening, and errors won't pile up.
  4. 04Backup version — Also export the work as a plain video file. If the real-time piece stops, you can at least play that.

What to bring on site

ItemWhy
Power strips, extension cordsThere are never enough outlets
Spare video cablesBad cables are the most common failure on site
Adapter kitEvery device has different ports
Black tape · cable tiesNeeded for both safety and looks
Install photos and settings notesLets you reproduce it exactly at the next show
Printed work labelOn-site labels are often late

Safety and liability

  1. 01Cables — Keep cables out of visitor paths. If unavoidable, use covers.
  2. 02Brightness and flashing — Rapid flashing can trigger seizures in some viewers. Avoid more than three flashes in 1 second, and if there are any, post a notice at the entrance.
  3. 03Sound — Set volume and direction so you don't affect neighboring works. Consider directional speakers or headphones.
  4. 04Cameras and privacy — If the work captures viewers on camera, state whether it records; if it doesn't store footage, say so.
Design for the last day of the show, not the first. Still running a month later is part of the work.13.5 Making it work in the gallery

13.6

Artwork statements and portfolios

In media art, writing is part of the work. Viewers read the statement to understand the piece, and open calls and residencies are judged on text and photos alone. The demands differ from a video portfolio, so practice separately.

Anatomy of a statement

01What it isStart with 'This work is …' Open with background and nobody reads to the end
02HowWhat happens when the viewer does what. The user manual for an interactive work
03WhyThe reason it must take this form. Grounds for a choice, not a tech showcase
TIP

Gallery wall texts of 150 to 300 characters get read. Any longer and nobody stands there reading.

Words to avoid

  • Vague big words — 'existence', 'relationship', 'boundary' used without explanation say nothing at all. If you use one, state concretely, at least once, what it refers to in this work.
  • Listing tech — tools belong in the materials line. Showing off tools in the statement makes the work look small.
  • Unverified quotations — don't quote books you haven't read. It's the first thing jurors ask about.

What goes in the portfolio

ItemContentCommon mistake
Key images1 installation view, 2 detailsScreen captures only
Documentation video1–2 min, including audience reactionsLong footage of the screen alone
Materials lineMedium, dimensions, duration, yearLeft out entirely
System diagram1 input-processing-output flowchartExplained in words only
Installation requirementsSpace, power, blackout needsJurors can't tell if it can be installed
ContributionYour share of a collaborationTeam work made to look solo

Documentation keeps the work alive

A media art work vanishes when the show closes. Only the documentation remains, and the next open call and the next exhibition are decided by it. During the show, be sure to shoot installation views, details, real audience reactions, and even the installation process.

The most important day in the gallery is not opening day but the day you shoot documentation.13.6 Artwork statements and portfolios

13.8

Three debates

Immersion, AI, preservation. Study media art and you will run into three debates. None has a settled answer, so the goal is to know the grounds of each position and decide where you stand.

Debate one — immersive exhibitions: art or product

The product view

  • One Korean study defines immersive exhibitions as 'commercial content aimed at the immersive experience itself, beyond mediating art'
  • Admission — immersive shows abroad charge adults tens of thousands to over a hundred thousand won; Korean public museums mostly around KRW 10,000
  • An industry CEO admits — 'anyone can take on a theme like waves, so borrowed ideas and copyright infringement are hard to prove'

The necessity view

  • When everything can be watched at home, immersive experience gives people a reason to show up in person
  • Public museums are absorbing it too — MMCA's 2026 exhibition on light, the Seoul Museum of Art's new-media branch

A question to consider

Is a work made on a KRW 1.5 million budget and shown four hours each evening on a city screen the same media art as a ticketed venue drawing millions a year? What divides them: the medium, the source of money, or where the audience stands?

Term

Two strands of authorship — work that uses AI as a tool differs from work that takes AI itself as its subject. It is the difference between using a tool and questioning it.

Debate two — generative AI and authorship

The 2024 gapThere were no rules

  • A generative AI entry won the grand prize in a public-service ad contest, then sparked controversy
  • The organizer said 'with no AI clause, we can neither block nor encourage it', and the award stood

The 2026 shiftDisclosure, not bans

  • JoongAng Media Art Competition — AI assistance encouraged, heavy use judged in a separate category, use and scope must be disclosed
  • Veterans Affairs ministry call — asks for tools used · scope · even reference sources
  • An international conference's art track — stating AI's role is mandatory

Work that questions the tool2026 Korea Artist Prize finalist

  • Restores a 1946 film with AI, revealing that 'technology cannot fully restore history'
  • Examines the biases built into video-generation models

In 2 years, 'AI use: no rules' became 'AI use: must be declared.' The habit of logging in your production journal which tools you used, where, and how much becomes your submission paperwork.

Debate three — how a work survives

737unitsDamaged CRTs in the restoration of The More, the Better, repaired or replaced with used parts
266unitsSmall monitors at the top, replaced with flat displays that keep the original look. Eight videos converted to digital
4 days a week, 2hoursOperating time since the September 2022 restart, to protect the work
  • The open question — is The More, the Better, with its CRTs swapped for flat screens, still the same work? Conservation guidelines divide installations into cases where 'the equipment is part of the work itself' and where it is 'used only to show the work.' In the first case, replacing equipment means changing the work.
  • What students can do now — document the software and versions, hardware, settings, and 'what may change and what must not,' and the work can be restaged years later.
  • Preservation as a career — in 2026 MMCA opened a training program for conservators, new media included. Not only making work but keeping it is becoming a profession.
On the day you finish a work, write a one-page tech spec. That sheet becomes the key to switching the work back on 10 years later.13.8 Three debates
