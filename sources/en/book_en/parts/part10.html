PART 10

Introduction

Video makes a fine hobby, but most readers of this book want to work in it. Then you need to understand the market's structure first. Knowing where the money flows, who gives work to whom, and which jobs are growing and which are shrinking is part of the skill.

The change to watch is that the center of gravity of video jobs has moved from broadcasting and film to digital, commerce and in-house corporate teams. Broadcasters' open recruitment was once the only gateway; now brand content teams, e-commerce companies' video staff and small studios hire far more newcomers. This shift has changed what goes into a portfolio, too.

Meanwhile, generative AI is driving down the rates for simple editing and simple graphics. The way to survive is not learning tools faster but moving up into judgment. Deciding what to shoot, explaining why this cut, and reaching agreement with the client are still human work.

Background 18

Where the value of video comes from

Many people and companies are involved before a video reaches its audience. Know where in that chain value is created, and you will see where you can stand. The traditional film industry was divided into development and financing, production, distribution and exhibition; after the theatrical release, a film came out in turn on video, pay TV and free-to-air broadcast. Staggering release dates by medium is called holdback, or windowing. It was a structure for earning from different audiences with the same work.

Streaming services shook this order hard. Works now skip theaters and go straight out worldwide, and more platforms finance and produce directly. YouTube and short-form platforms opened a way for individual creators to skip financing and distribution and meet audiences directly. As production costs fall and distribution barriers disappear, the value of video is shifting more and more to 'who can gather an audience.'

A key concept in this shift is intellectual property, or IP. When one story and its characters expand from webtoon to drama, games and merchandise, the value multiplies. Korean content draws attention worldwide not just through individual hits but through the power of its stories, characters and worlds to carry over into other media.

Seen from the production floor, value doesn't come from technical skill alone. An ad's value comes from solving the brand's problem, an educational video's from helping learners understand, a creator video's from trust with a particular audience. When two people with the same shooting skills command different fees, the reason is usually this difference in problem-solving.

A useful frame for looking at the industry is the value chain. Ask who bears the risk and who takes the profit at each stage: planning and development, production, post-production, distribution, exhibition and consumption. Traditionally, investors and production companies bore the risk, and profit was recouped in distribution and exhibition. In the platform era, much of the risk has shifted to the platforms; in exchange, more production companies take a stable production fee and give up extra earnings tied to performance.

An individual creator's income comes in four streams: commissioned freelance work, a share of platform ad revenue, direct brand partnerships, and your own business, such as products and teaching. The earlier streams are predictable; the later ones swing widely but have a higher ceiling. Rely on just one and you will be shaken when the market shifts, so it is safer to try at least two from early in your career.

Further reading — Harold Vogel, Entertainment Industry Economics · Chris Anderson, The Long Tail

T-shaped skills

Deep in one thing, with an understanding of the whole process. That is the shape of skill the market wants now.

Jobs for people who can only edit are shrinking. Jobs for people who can write a proposal, shoot, edit and read the results are growing.

TIP

The international standard is a showreel of about 1 minute with your role captioned on each clip.

  • If you are considering jobs or clients abroad, prepare an English showreel and résumé in advance.

Background 19

What a price pays for

The figure on a quote is not simply the cost of the gear, nor of the hours. A production budget usually splits into three blocks: people's time, equipment and locations, and rights. Beginners most often forget the third. The same video is priced differently for 1 year of in-house training than for 6 months on national TV. The wider the media, term, and territory, the higher the price, and if the quote leaves the scope unstated, the work can end up used without limit.

People's time is not just the shoot day. A project runs from planning meetings and research, script and storyboard, casting and scouting, and the shoot, through editing and revisions, to delivery and invoicing. The less experienced you are, the less time you allow for editing and revisions, yet that is where the most time disappears. The standard defense is a clause that fixes the number of revisions and bills anything beyond as extra.

There are three ways to set a price: hours times an hourly rate, the going rate for similar projects, or the value the client gains. As your career grows, the third carries more weight, because even at the same 30 seconds, a small shop's intro video and the main cut of a national campaign solve problems of very different size. You can price this way only if you can explain what problem your work solves.

In Korea, the standard contracts and industry surveys published by the Ministry of Culture, Sports and Tourism, the Korea Creative Content Agency (KOCCA), and others serve as benchmarks. A standard contract covers payment timing, copyright ownership, scope of revisions, and termination terms, which makes it a good skeleton for a first contract. Amounts change every year, so check the latest figures, but the structure of the items changes little.

Finally, a low price does not bring the next job. Start too cheap and it gets hard to refuse revision requests, and that time is lost to other work. You may take a low fee early on for the experience, but even then, write down the scope and number of revisions clearly. Guarding your price is guarding your time.

Further reading — KOCCA, standard contracts and industry surveys · Ministry of Culture, Sports and Tourism, standard contracts for popular culture and arts · Chris Do, “Sell Value, Not Price”

Seven things to check in any contract

Note

90% of disputes come from revision counts and usage scope. Put both in numbers.

  1. 01Scope of work: state in numbers how many videos, how many minutes, how many versions.
  2. 02Revisions: unlimited revisions cannot exist. Usually cap them at 2–3 and set a fee for more.
  3. 03Copyright: make clear whether it is a full transfer or a license, and settle whether you may show the work in your portfolio.
  4. 04Usage scope: specify media, term, and territory. Online-only and all-media are priced differently.
  5. 05Payment terms: set the split and timing of deposit, interim, and final payments.
  6. 06Delay and termination: decide how costs are handled when the client postpones the shoot.
  7. 07Supplied materials: write down who provides products, logos, and fonts, and by when.

Always include a portfolio clause

Under a no-disclosure contract, even your best work can't help you win the next job. At the very least, negotiate the right to show it after a set period. For newcomers and freelancers, this clause matters more than the rate.

A low price doesn't bring the next job. A quote with no stated scope only brings more work.Background 19 What a price pays for

9.6

Career paths by role

Your first role is not your final identity. Here are year-by-year paths for six roles, and the four things that set how fast you grow.

EditingFrom rough cuts to head of post

  • Years 1–2: rough cuts, captions, sorting cuts
  • Years 3–5: owning structure and rhythm, color grading
  • Year 6+: lead editor, post-production supervisor

CameraFrom second camera to DP

  • Years 1–2: gear care, second camera
  • Years 3–5: main camera, lighting coordination
  • Year 6+: DP, look design

DirectingFrom AD to director

  • Years 1–2: AD, running the set
  • Years 3–5: directing solo, leading planning
  • Year 6+: director, creative director

Motion graphicsFrom templates to art director

  • Years 1–2: template work, caption design
  • Years 3–5: title sequences, 3D
  • Year 6+: art director

Content marketingFrom metrics reports to brand lead

  • Years 1–2: posting and metrics reports
  • Years 3–5: channel strategy, campaign design
  • Year 6+: head of brand content

XR planningFrom research to winning contracts

  • Years 1–2: research, documentation
  • Years 3–5: exhibition planning, vendor management
  • Year 6+: lead planner, winning contracts

What sets your growth speed

TIP

Directing begins the day an editor starts listening in on planning meetings.

  1. 01Volume. The gap between someone who makes 100 pieces in 1 year and someone who makes 10 can't be closed 3 years later.
  2. 02Feedback frequency. Working alone, you don't improve. Skill is the number of times you show your work and get critiqued.
  3. 03The habit of doing the next level's work ahead of time.
  4. 04Records. Only those who note what they did and why can improve their judgment.

Career switches happen more often than you think

Moving from editing to directing, from camera to directing, or from content marketing to brand planning is common. Don't treat your first role as your final identity.
