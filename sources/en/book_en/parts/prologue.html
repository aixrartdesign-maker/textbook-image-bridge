Frame and aspect ratio

The frame is the border formed by the screen's four edges; the aspect ratio is the relationship between its width and height. These two decide, before anything else, what goes in and what gets cut.

Aspect ratio is written as width divided by height. 16:9 is 1.78; CinemaScope is 2.39. Vertical video flips the same numbers and is called 9:16.

Term

Safe area — the inner zone for key information, in case the screen edges are cropped or covered by UI. Broadcast kept the outer 10% clear; today the spots covered by platform buttons and captions play that role.

Decide the ratio before you start shooting: shoot horizontal and crop to vertical, and 2/3 of the frame is gone. If you must deliver in several ratios, keep the subject centered with room on both sides.

Task

Tip
Don't move the subject; only turn the camera. What the ratio changes is not the subject but how much of the surroundings you see.

Resolution · frame rate · shutter

How dense each picture is, how many are shown in 1 second, how long each was exposed. These are the three numbers that set the texture of video.

1080p1920×1080, FHD. The delivery default
4KUHD3840×2160, four times the area of FHD
24fpsThe convention for film and brand films
30fpsBroadcast · explainer video; 60fps for sports · slow motion

ResolutionNumber of dots

  • Width×height in pixels. 1920×1080 is about 2.07 million
  • Higher stays crisp even when shown large, but files get bigger
  • Choose to suit screen size and viewing distance

Frame rateFrames per second

  • Written as fps. 24 for film, 30 for broadcast, 60 for sports and games
  • Shoot high and play back slow for slow motion
  • Keep one value throughout a project

ShutterExposure time per frame

  • Slow shutter speed makes motion smear; fast makes it stutter
  • 180-degree rule — shutter speed at twice the frame rate (for 24fps, 1/48 s)
  • The baseline for natural motion blur
TIP

Shoot in 4K and deliver in FHD, and the edit gains room to punch in or recrop for vertical. That reframing room can only be created at the shoot.

Set the three numbers once before the shoot and keep them to the end. Change them midway and frames double up or drop out in the edit, and image quality turns uneven. Delivery specs come first; work the camera settings backward from them.

Exercise0.2
Task
Shoot the same movement once at 24fps and once at 60fps, play them side by side, and write one sentence on which looks 'cinematic' and which looks 'lifelike.'
Tip
Set the shutter speed to 1/48 s and 1/120 s respectively, and you see the difference made by frame rate alone.

Cut · scene · sequence · take

These are the units for counting video. If the set and the edit suite don't use the same words, people picture different things for the same scene.

01FrameA single still picture. A 15-second ad at 24fps is 360 frames
02Cut (shot)One unbroken run of the camera. A 15-second ad usually has 6–12 cuts
03SceneA group of cuts at the same place · same time. Counted as the kitchen scene, the street scene
04SequenceA group of scenes forming one event. A 15-second ad is usually a single problem–solution sequence

Cut — the unit of editing

  • One continuous stretch of picture in the finished piece
  • What the editor cuts and joins
  • "Lots of cuts" means a fast editing rhythm

Take — the unit of shooting

  • One attempt at shooting a cut. Take 1, take 2
  • Every "one more" on set adds a take
  • Editing means choosing one of several takes

Coverage means shooting a scene broken into several sizes and angles. Only with a wide cut showing everything, a medium cut on the people and close-ups of faces and hands does the edit suite have choices. A scene shot without coverage can't be edited; it has to be reshot.

Exercise0.3
Task
Pick a 15-second ad you like, count its cuts, and make a table of how many scenes it divides into.
Tip
Pause every time the picture changes. Consecutive cuts in the same place belong to one scene.

Horizontal, vertical and platform specs

The same video gets a different ratio, length and covered area depending on where it is posted. The platform is not the last variable in planning; it is the first condition.

Note

Platforms change length limits and UI layout often. Recheck the platform's current specs right before delivery.

Horizontal video often moves from the situation in to the person; vertical often starts with the person and adds the situation after. To make both formats from the same material, don't just crop; restructure.

Reading order for this book

This book puts practice ahead of theory. First you learn to read the screen in the language of video, decide what to shoot through planning (storyboard · PPM), then finish a piece through shooting, lighting · sound and editing, in that order. Next come media specs, ad cases, platforms, industry · careers and the AI shift, and finally a return to history, theory and media art. History and theory read after you have made something with your own hands become things you can explain, not things to memorize.
