PART 05

Introduction

Shooting gathers material; editing makes meaning. Change the order and length and the same footage yields an entirely different video. Kuleshov proved this 100 years ago, and it works even harder in the short-form era of ever-shorter cuts.

This part starts from the history of editing technology and lays out Premiere Pro and After Effects features systematically. The feature lists are a map, not something to memorize. Know which area of which tool a problem belongs to and one search solves it; without a map, you wander for hours.

After Effects in particular thinks differently from Premiere. Premiere handles time horizontally; After Effects handles layers vertically. Trying to cut in After Effects without knowing this is the classic beginner's mistake. Simply splitting the two tools' roles halves your working time.

Background 7

Invisible editing

Good editing keeps the audience from noticing the editing. A cut slices time and space, yet the audience must feel it as a natural flow.

Editor Walter Murch's In the Blink of an Eye (1995) likens a blink to a cut. We blink when a thought changes or our gaze shifts. A good cut likewise falls where one paragraph of the viewer's thought ends. So the editor looks first not at the screen but at where the audience's mind has reached.

Murch ranked the criteria for a cut: emotion 51 percent, story 23 percent, rhythm 10 percent, eye-trace 7 percent, the 2D plane of the screen 5 percent, 3D spatial continuity 4 percent. The order matters more than the numbers: pick the cut with the right emotion even if continuity slips a little. Student work often flips this order, clinging to matched action and losing the emotional flow.

Continuity techniques — cutting on action, eyeline match, the 180-degree rule — all keep the audience from getting lost in space and time. Eisenstein's montage tradition did the opposite, exposing the seams to create collision and meaning. The editor chooses, case by case, to hide the seams or show them.

Editing is also an art of time. Shorter cuts raise tension; longer ones give the audience room to think. Cuts in ads and short-form speed up not just to pack in information but to keep catching the viewer's attention afresh.

Digital nonlinear editing made everything undoable, but it does not supply judgment. Even while learning the software, make a habit of asking at every cut, 'What is the audience feeling now?' Further reading: Walter Murch, In the Blink of an Eye; Ken Dancyger, The Technique of Film and Video Editing.

5.2

Basic editing principles

Cut not because it's dull but when the next cut offers better information.

TIP

Lay sound first. Set the timing of music and dialogue, then lay picture on top, and the finish improves.

  1. 01Cut only for a reason: when the next cut gives better information.
  2. 02Cut mid-action. Cut after the action ends and the rhythm sags.
  3. 03Lay sound first. Set the music and dialogue timing, then add picture.
  4. 04Change size and angle together. Change only one and it looks like a jump.
  5. 05Keep eyelines. If a character looking left looks right in the next cut, the relationship breaks.
  6. 06Throw out boldly. Keep a shot just because it was hard won and the video drags.
  7. 07Lock the opening and ending first. Once they are set, the middle sorts itself out.
  8. 08Rewatch a day after finishing. Slack you couldn't see that day will show.

Working order for 3D text and lighting

TIP

Match the camera's focal length to the live-action lens, or the perspective won't line up.

  1. 01Choose the typeface. Tight tracking makes faces collide in 3D extrusion, so track wide and go bold.
  2. 02Make the text layer 3D and set extrusion depth. 15–30% of letter height is safe.
  3. 03Set the material. Metal: reflections and highlights; matte: mostly diffuse.
  4. 04Add a camera. Match its focal length to the live-action lens.
  5. 05Place lights. Check the shadow direction in the footage and set the key light at the same angle.
  6. 06Make a floor layer to catch shadows. Without it, the text looks like a sticker floating in air.
  7. 07Add motion. By default, ease out for entrances, ease in for exits.
  8. 08Turn on motion blur and match the grain to the live action.

Background 8

The illusion of life
— origins of the animation principles

How to breathe weight, character and emotion into still drawings was worked out over decades of trial and error. The principles of animation are physics and acting theory at once.

In 1914, Winsor McCay's Gertie the Dinosaur showed a character taking orders and reacting before an audience, proving animation could create a being with personality. Preparing Snow White and the Seven Dwarfs (1937), the Walt Disney Studio had its animators observe real animals and people in motion and taught them drawing and anatomy.

Disney's core animators of the era were called the 'Nine Old Men.' Two of them, Frank Thomas and Ollie Johnston, distilled the studio's principles into twelve in The Illusion of Life (1981): squash and stretch, anticipation, staging, pose to pose, follow-through, slow in and slow out, arcs, secondary action, timing, exaggeration, solid drawing, appeal. These are less rules than ways of translating observed reality so the audience feels it more.

Half the principles are physics. A ball squashing as it lands and stretching as it bounces, a heavy object starting and stopping slowly — these make mass and inertia visible. The other half is acting. Anticipation signals the next action, exaggeration clarifies emotion, and appeal makes the audience give their heart to the character.

In 1987, Pixar's John Lasseter presented "Principles of Traditional Animation Applied to 3D Computer Animation" at SIGGRAPH. His point: the tools changed from pencil to computer, but how audiences sense life in motion did not. A motion designer easing a logo in the Graph Editor today follows the same principles.

Before memorizing the principles, build the habit of watching movement around you. An eye for the order in which a person rises from a chair or a ball rolls down stairs makes the principles living tools. Further reading: Frank Thomas · Ollie Johnston, The Illusion of Life; Richard Williams, The Animator's Survival Kit.

Compositing and keying in practice

Judge keying in six steps; when a composite shows, look to three causes: black level · atmospheric perspective · grain.

StepWhat to doCriterion
Garbage matteMask out what lies outside the greenLight stands and floor edge removed
Primary keyKey out the screen colorHair still intact
Matte cleanupClean up viewing alpha in black and whiteSubject pure white, background pure black
Spill removalRemove green on skin and clothesSkin hasn't turned gray
Edge treatmentChoke and feather edgesNo jaggies when zoomed in
Color matchingMatch background color and brightnessLight direction, color temp match

Three reasons a composite shows

Cause 1Black level

  • If an element's blacks are darker than the live action, it jars at once
  • Lift the blacks slightly

Cause 2Atmospheric perspective

  • Distance lowers contrast and mixes in background color
  • Lower saturation and contrast with distance

Cause 3Grain and noise

  • Live action has noise; CG has none
  • Add matching grain to the elements
Final check

Shrink the image or switch to black and white. Mismatches show more on a small screen and in black and white.

5.13

Types of animation

Animation is divided by what each frame is made from, and broadly splits into 2D and 3D. Change the material, and the texture of the motion and the way it is made change with it.

Features of each method

CelCel animation

  • Characters drawn on clear celluloid, shot layered over backgrounds
  • The standard for commercial features; the origin of digital layers

CuttingCutout

  • Figures cut from paper or cloth, joints pinned, moved bit by bit and shot
  • Lotte Reiniger's The Adventures of Prince Achmed (1926); now done with digital puppets

SandSand animation

  • Sand on a lightbox pushed by hand into new shapes and shot
  • Known for morphing: one shape flows into the next scene

PeoplePixilation

  • People shot one frame at a time like puppets
  • Unreal motion — gliding or floating in midair; Norman McLaren's Neighbours (1952)

PaperPaper · drawing

  • The most basic method: drawing on paper, one sheet at a time

One-page summary

5.1 A history of editing technology

The tools changed, but proxies, EDLs, timecode and a feel for frames remain.

5.2 Basic principles of editing

Cut not because it's boring but when the next shot gives better information, and cut mid-action. Lay the sound first.

5.3 Comparing editing software

Premiere and After Effects are the hiring standard. Know both deeply.

5.4 · 5.5 Premiere and After Effects

Premiere handles time horizontally; After Effects handles layers vertically. Split the roles and you halve the time.

5.6 The 12 principles of animation

Fix easing, anticipation, follow-through and exaggeration, in that order, and the beginner look disappears.

5.7 Compositing and keying

A composite floats for three reasons: black levels, atmospheric perspective and grain.

5.8 Color grading

Exposure and white balance first, the look after. Lift · gamma · gain adjust shadows · midtones · highlights.

5.9 Automation tools

Automated output is a draft. A person checks proper nouns, pacing, fingers and lettering.

5.10 · 5.11 Export and expressions

Stick to the right codec for each purpose, and cut keyframes with wiggle · loopOut · time · linear.

5.12 · 5.13 Resolve and animation

One job per node. Choose the method by texture first, budget second. Quality comes from the order of cuts, not the number of transitions.

Questions to consider

What makes me decide to cut in my edits?
Is it boredom, or because the next shot gives better information? Write one line on why you dropped each cut, and your criterion emerges.
When you feel like adding a transition, what does that spot actually need?
First check whether it really needs an effect, or just a different order of cuts.

Agree on project folder structure and file naming on day one. Save frequently used titles and effects as presets (over two months, that saves a day). Always take revision requests by timecode, and when feedback is "it's just not quite there overall," send it back and ask for specifics.
