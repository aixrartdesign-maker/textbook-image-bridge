Background 22

What gets expensive when tools get cheap

In 1965, Intel co-founder Gordon Moore published his observation that the number of components on an integrated circuit doubles at regular intervals. What matters in this rule of thumb, known as Moore's law, is not the number but the consequence. When a resource keeps getting cheaper, people change how they work so as to use it freely. Economists describe this as complements rising in value. When one thing gets cheap, what is used alongside it becomes more valuable.

For the past 100 years, the most expensive thing in video production was 'the cost of one more take.' Every foot of film, every day of cast and crew, cost money. So every production practice, from storyboards to rehearsals to test shoots, was designed to reduce failure. When digital shooting eliminated the cost of film, the first thing to change on set was the number of takes.

Generative tools go one step further. The cost of making a draft has fallen close to 0. Getting three concept images once meant days of an illustrator's time, so you thought long about which three to ask for. Now you can see 100 in 30 minutes. The hard thinking has moved from 'what to ask for' to 'what to choose.'

So what got expensive? First, judgment. The ability to tell which of 100 images fits the brand and will work for the audience cannot be generated. Second, trust. When anyone can make a plausible image, what clients pay for is not the image but the assurance that 'hand it to this person and it gets done, responsibly.' Third, the real thing. The value of shooting what cannot be generated, such as real products, real stores and real people's faces, has actually gone up.

Knowing this structure also makes clear where students should spend their time. You can learn to operate a tool in a few days. But the ability to say why a cut works, the trust that comes from meeting deadlines and the eye that reads light on set respond only to accumulation. There is one yardstick for reading the numbers in the sections ahead: what did this change make cheap, and so what did it make valuable?

Further reading — Carl Shapiro · Hal Varian, Information Rules · Erik Brynjolfsson · Andrew McAfee, The Second Machine Age · Ethan Mollick, Co-Intelligence

11.2

Reshaping the pipeline

How has the pipeline been rebuilt? Traditional production flowed one way: planning → shooting → post-production. With generative tools the flow has become a loop, and settling the concept and making the final images now happen at the same stage.

01PromptWrite references and conditions in words
02GenerateMake many variants at once
03SelectPick what meets the criteria
04ReviseFix the prompt, back to 01
Term

'Post-production is the new pre-production.' A line from Coca-Cola's making-of video that sums up a structure in which decisions stay open to the end.

The two structures compared

Traditional linear structure

  • Order — planning → shooting → post
  • Undo — no going back a stage
  • Decisions — mostly locked before the shoot
  • Bottleneck — shoot schedule and budget
  • Risk — miss it on set and it's over
  • Control needed — advance planning

AI iterative structure

  • Order — a generate ↔ select ↔ regenerate loop
  • Undo — regenerate any time
  • Decisions — open to the end
  • Bottleneck — selection and quality control
  • Risk — endless revisions stretch the schedule
  • Control needed — stop criteria and selection criteria

Work that grew and work that shrank

New rolesGeneration specialist · workflow designer · curator · reviewer

  • Curation that filters mass output
  • Brand and legal review
  • Orchestration that ties many tools into one reproducible flow

Shrinking workSimple repetitive tasks

  • Basic rotoscoping and cleanup
  • Banner size variants, repetitive asset production
  • In a US agency survey, over half of firms cut or stopped entry-level hiring

Unchanged workJudgment and coordination

  • Supervising, art direction
  • Decision-making with the client
  • Judging key cuts and continuity from cut to cut

Three safeguards that keep the schedule intact

  1. 01Stop criteria — Decide in advance: 'after N generations, choose from those.' Without a rule, you keep making more even after a good result.
  2. 02Locked zones — First lock what must not change, such as the product, logo and key message, then vary the rest.
  3. 03Version log — Record which prompts and references produced which results. Work you can't reproduce can't be remade when revision requests come.
Generation is infinite; schedules are not. Before you start, write down 'how many to make before stopping.'11.2 Reshaping the pipeline
Exercise11.2

Tip
Boxes and arrows are all a flowchart needs. Be sure to draw the arrows that loop back.
Assessment

Model answer
A plan stating counts and criteria together, like 'round 1: generate 30, pick 3; round 2: up to 10 from the picks.'

Where in the process it is used

Which stages actually use it? Surveys show generative tools are used most not to make a piece from start to finish, but to turn what already exists into many versions.

42%#1 generative AI use in the IAB survey — versions by audience (visual style changes 38%, contextual fit 36%)
About 80typesAd formats Cheil Worldwide's in-house tool auto-generates from one asset. Over 90% saved per banner (company's own claim)
48.6%2026 survey of US video producers — substantial generative AI use in VFX. Shooting · capture 13.3%, camera · lighting design 8.3%
Term

Ad multiplier — keeping a high-performing ad's cut structure and pacing while swapping only cast · wardrobe · location · props to mass-produce variants. Common for regional versions.

Where AI has moved in

  • Variants and localization — audience versions, format variants, ad multipliers
  • VFX execution — repetitive work such as rotoscoping, cleanup, plate prep, denoising, upscaling and texture generation
  • Planning and previz — concept art, storyboards, previsualization. The stage that changed most as draft costs fell close to 0
  • Automated review — one global food company auto-checks about 16 compliance items on its own platform

Where people still do the work

  • Shoot design — tools cluster in post and have barely reached camera and lighting design. Why this book gave pages to lighting · lenses · composition
  • Brand assets — exact logos and product shapes aren't guaranteed at generation. Even tool makers advise 'if accuracy matters, add it in post'
  • Strategy and judgment — agencies rate their AI maturity as intermediate in media planning and creative direction, beginner in strategic planning and research analysis

11.4

People, digital doubles, consent

AI actors, digital doubles and the question of consent. Generative tools proved useful fastest with backgrounds and props; the area that stays hard longest is people. Because audiences have trained all their lives to read faces, people are a technical problem and a rights problem at once.

Three approaches

Fully generated personSomeone who doesn't exist, made from scratch

  • No likeness-rights issues, low cost
  • Hard to keep one face across cuts
  • Weak at subtle facial acting

Digital doubleA scanned copy of a real actor

  • Dangerous scenes, age changes, fixes without reshoots
  • Actor consent and a usage-scope contract are a must

Live action + partial generationShoot the people, generate only backgrounds · props

  • The most widely used on ad sets today
  • Safest on both quality and rights
TIP

The best reference image is a single front view with flat lighting and a neutral expression. The more distinctive features, the harder they are to hold.

Keeping the same face

A face that changes from cut to cut is the most common failure. In practice, you make the reference first and match everything else to it.

01Reference imageLock one front view first
02Reference inputFeed the reference into every later generation; prompt only the angle · lighting · pose
03Character sheetFront · profile · three-quarter · full body on one sheet, like an animation model sheet
04Fix in postClose the remaining gaps with compositing and color correction. Don't expect 100% from generation

Rights: four things to check first

  1. 01Likeness — using a real person's face · voice · signature gestures requires their consent. Creating a look-alike can also be a problem.
  2. 02Scope — specify media · term · territory · extent of edits. 'Consent to be filmed' and 'consent to AI training and generation' are separate items.
  3. 03Disclosure — when a generated person is shown as if real, it is safest to label it so no one is misled.
  4. 04Records — keep the model used, the prompts, and the sources of reference images. Disputes usually escalate when there are no records.
Shoot for real any cut that needs a human face in close-up. Spending the time generation saved you right there is the best deal you will make.11.4 People, digital doubles, consent

11.5

Sets, LED walls, generated backgrounds

From sets to LED walls, and on to generated backgrounds. In 100 years the way backgrounds are made has changed three times: build it or go there, put an image up behind, or composite it in post-production. Generative tools have sharply cut the cost of the third and changed how material for the second is made.

Four options and how to choose

MethodStrengthsWeaknessesWhen to choose
Real locationThe most convincing light and textureWeather · permits · travel costsWhen the brand is selling authenticity
Built setTotal controlCost and build timeRepeat shoots, series
LED wall (virtual production)Final image checked on set; reflections really landGear · pre-production costs, moiréSubjects where reflections matter: cars · glass · metal
Chroma key + generated backgroundCheapest and fastestEdges and light matching are hardPeople-centered, tight budget
TIP

The right order: settle the background before the shoot, then set the lights to match its direction. Locked-off shots suit generated backgrounds best.

Four places a generated background gives itself away

Light directionLight on the person, shadows in the background

  • If they disagree, it shows at once

Color temperatureA daylight-lit person on an evening background

  • Looks off even with a clean composite

Perspective and lensVanishing point and angle of view

  • Put a telephoto-compressed background behind a wide-angle person and the space breaks

MovementCamera parallax

  • When the camera moves, the background must move with matching parallax

Bringing in a whole space

Shooting a real space in many photos or clips, rebuilding it as 3D data, then moving the camera freely inside it has entered everyday practice. Photogrammetry has been joined by methods that learn and reproduce even the brightness of light rays, so subjects that were hard to rebuild, like glass · metal · foliage, are now workable. Shoot a store for half a day and every later ad can be shot in that space: the shoot itself becomes an asset.

Saying you will decide the background later means saying you will decide the lighting later. The background comes first.11.5 Sets, LED walls, generated backgrounds
Exercise11.5
Task

Tip
When shooting the person, capture one take with a gray ball and a white ball in frame. Matching light direction and color temperature gets much easier.
Assessment

A case that picked the background first, lit to match its light, then shot, so the composite needed almost no correction.

11.6

Elements and particles

Why is simulation still here? Generative models are good at 'motion they have seen often,' but water pouring, cloth draping and smoke curling along a wall are still unstable. These are the results of physical laws, and a generative model does not calculate laws; it makes pictures that look similar.

How the two differ

AspectSimulationGenerative model
How it worksSolves physics equationsGenerates look-alike images from learned patterns
RepeatabilitySame settings, same resultDifferent every time
RevisionsChange one variable, recalculateRegenerate everything
Product fidelityKeeps the real product's exact shapeShape drifts little by little
TimeLong setup, long computeTens of seconds to minutes
Good atLiquids · cloth · debris · collisionsAtmosphere, smoke, dust, background elements
Note

Brand assets may not be distorted. In a cut of a drink being poured into a glass, if the label warps for even one frame, the cut is unusable.

When ads especially need simulation

  • When the product is the hero — no distortion of label or shape is allowed.
  • Repeat delivery — when one scene ships in versions for many countries, a different result every time means no control.
  • Exact timing — a request like a droplet landing at 0.4 seconds to hit the music can only be met by calculation.

Realistic ways to mix

Split into layersCalculate the product, generate the background

  • Simulate the product and key physics
  • Generate background dust · haze · light bloom and composite it

Generated output as elementsDon't hand over the whole frame

  • Render generated smoke or fire as element footage for compositing

Generate the concept, calculate the finalSeparate the pitch from the final

  • Pitch drafts fast, by generation
  • After approval, redo the final as a simulation
If a brand logo or the product's shape is on screen, don't leave that part to generation. The rest you can.11.6 Elements and particles
Exercise11.6

One piece of evidence on physics

29.5/100Score of the best video-generation model on a physics-understanding benchmark published in 2026 (real footage = 100). How realistic a clip looked had no meaningful correlation with its physics score
Spotlight

Recent research on improving fluid behavior trains on ground-truth data made with existing simulation software. Simulation is not being replaced; it has moved upstream of the generative model.

The researchers' conclusion is simple. Looking plausible does not mean understanding physics.

Mixing is the default

  1. 01Split the layers — shoot or simulate the product and key physics, generate background atmosphere and particles, and unify compositing and color in post-production.
  2. 02Separate pitch from final — generate pitch drafts fast; after approval, remake the final with a method you can control.
  3. 03Settle disclosure early — before delivery, list which stages used generative tools. Platforms and clients ask for it.
If brand assets are on screen, control that layer. The rest can go to generation.11.7 Traditional production and AI production

Generations, experience, process — what the film The Intern shows

A 70-year-old intern and a 30-year-old founder

Nancy Meyers's The Intern (2015) tells of a retired 70-year-old man who joins an online fashion startup as a senior intern and works beside its young founder. What he brings is not the latest tools but the experience of having seen how a company runs from start to finish: a feel for process. The Korean remake, directed by Kim Do-young and starring Choi Min-sik · Han So-hee, opened on September 16, 2026. In a market where the entrance for newcomers has narrowed, there is one way to watch this film. Speed with tools is soon matched, but someone who knows the whole process is useful in any seat.

Four ways to survive a market with fewer entry-level jobs

Fewer scheduled open recruitments and more rolling · experienced hiring: this is not a video-industry trend alone. As more postings look for 'newcomers with experience,' work you have seen through to the end for a real purpose becomes your ticket in. On that premise, I recommend four things.

ProcessThe value of knowing the whole

  • Anyone who has gone from planning to delivery · invoicing even once reads what comes before and after, whatever stage they join
  • This is exactly what the hero of The Intern has

AccumulationStack up many small finished works

  • Ten finished 15-second pieces beat one unfinished epic
  • Videos with a real purpose, for a local shop or a department event, are your track record

PathsFounding solo or as a team

  • With gear and generative tools cheaper, one person can handle planning · production · delivery
  • Two or three classmates splitting roles to start a small studio is also a realistic option

Student vision and expertise

What vision and expertise should students have? There is no answer to 'what should I learn to be safe.' Knowing how to operate a tool goes stale in 2–3 years at most. The better question is 'what abilities hold their value whatever changes.'

Generative tools produce averagely plausible results very fast. So the value of average output quickly converges to 0, and what rises in value is judgment that departs from the average: the ability to explain 'why it has to be this.'

Four things that hold their value

Problem definitionTranslate symptoms into causes

  • Turn 'make us a video' into 'we need more first visits from new customers'

Grounds for judgmentWhy this one out of ten

  • Ten drafts take a few minutes
  • Whoever can say why they chose becomes the one in charge

Feel for the realAn eye for the lies in generated images

  • Cuts with mismatched shadow directions, cuts whose depth of field can't be explained
  • Only people who have set lights and swapped lenses catch them

Language skillsProposals · director's notes · feedback · prompts

  • Writing the picture in your head so people and machines understand it the same way is productivity itself
Term

Broom-shaped — the T-shape (deep in one thing, understanding adjacent fields) with a wider crossbar. Think of an editor who also handles motion graphics · sound cleanup · thumbnails, or a DP who also proposes a rough cut and color-correction standards.

The shape of expertise: from T to broom

As AI tools lower the barriers to adjacent fields, the broom shape with its wider crossbar has become the realistic model. But a wider crossbar does not make the vertical stroke unnecessary. Someone who does everything passably ends up competing with tools that do everything passably. Pick one field you can answer for, and be able to show its depth as evidence.

What was required before AI

  • Tools — skill in specific software
  • Work — executing assigned cuts accurately
  • Speed — working fast alone
  • Evidence — finished output
  • Collaboration — communicating within your stage

What is required now

  • Tools — how fast you switch tools and why you choose them
  • Work — proposing what to make and owning it
  • Speed — quickly comparing · selecting many drafts
  • Evidence — output + the process of choosing
  • Collaboration — reading the whole pipeline, requesting · coordinating
Before you put a tool's name on your résumé, write one sentence about a judgment you made with it. If you can't, it isn't yours yet.11.11 Student vision and expertise

Key message and copy

Where does the emotional punch come from? After a good ad, what people remember is not the image but one sentence. The more easily generative tools make images, the more a work's level depends on whether it has a sentence to drive home.

A key message is a choice, not a summary

A key message means dropping nine of the ten things you could say and keeping just one. If dropping them hurts, it isn't decided yet. Test it three ways.

  1. 01One-sentence rule — it must finish as one sentence with no conjunctions. If 'and' or 'also' creeps in, you are saying two things.
  2. 02The opposite test — Nobody ever says the opposite of 'We care about our customers.' A sentence with no possible opposite is not a message.
  3. 03The subject swap — If the line still works with a competitor's name in place of the brand's, it belongs to no one.
TIP

In a 15-second ad, the turn usually comes at 8–10 seconds. It takes nerve not to fill the last second with explanation.

What moves us

Being moved doesn't come from explaining emotion; it comes when the audience notices on their own. Instead of naming grief, you show the empty chair left at the table.

SpecificsNumbers, things, time

  • Not 'many people' but 'a 3-shift worker at six a.m., just off duty'

TurnWhere expectation breaks once

  • The story's 'turn'
  • In a 15-second ad, at 8–10 seconds

SpaceRoom left for the audience to fill

  • Leave the conclusion unsaid

Five steps to writing copy

01Gather facts20 verified facts only, no adjectives
02Pick one personOne sentence on what is bothering one specific person right now
03Choose what to sayOf the 20, the one that matters only to that person
0430 sentencesSay it 30 ways. The first five usually get tossed
05Read aloudA line that doesn't roll off the tongue won't be remembered

The at-a-glance proposal

The power of planning comes from a document that gets read. Most student proposals are either pretty but unclear about what they propose, or full of content but unreadable. Reviewers and clients don't spend 3 minutes on one.

One-line definition

This video makes [whom] feel [what] so that they [take what action]. If you can't fill in this sentence, it isn't a plan yet.

Planning starts by narrowing

The essence of planning is not generating ideas but setting scope. Write down the three constraints of budget, schedule and crew before you start, and the ideas actually get better.

  • A not-to-do list — Spell out the subjects you won't cover, the techniques you won't use and the information you won't include. Without it, revision requests multiply endlessly.
  • Success criteria — Write them as results, not completion. They must be checkable numbers: not 'views' but '40% watched to the end.'
TIP

State the conclusion on the first page and add evidence as you go. It is a proposal, not a report.

Structure of an at-a-glance proposal

PageContentsLengthWhy it fails
CoverOne-line title and one-line definition1 pageA title but no definition
ProblemWhat is wrong right now1 pageDescribes the company, not the problem
SolutionSo what will you make1 pageLists several options and asks them to pick
Tone3–6 reference images1–2 pagesPretty pictures, no reasons

Five ways to make it read at a glance

  1. 01One page, one message — The title is the conclusion. 'Market analysis' fails; 'Men in their 20s drop off in the first 3 seconds' succeeds.
  2. 02One big number — Make only the most important number big; keep the rest small.
  3. 03Show by comparison — Current vs. proposed, competitor vs. us, before and after. A comparison conveys the difference without explanation.
  4. 04Design the eye path — Pages read from top left to bottom right. Put the most important thing at top left.
  5. 05Don't write what you'll say — The slide is the evidence; your voice does the persuading.
When the proposal is done, read only the page titles in a row. If they tell the story on their own, it passes.11.13 The at-a-glance proposal

How to show work made with AI

Hiding the use of generative tools is no longer the safe choice. What matters is not whether you used them but where and how, and what a human decided.

One-page summary

11.1 How much production time has shrunk

Generation cost and time shrank; selection time didn't. Give figures with source and conditions.

11.2 How the pipeline was rebuilt

From linear to a generate↔select↔regenerate loop. Stop criteria · locked sections · version logs protect the schedule.

11.3 Where in the process it is actually used

Concentrated in variants · localization and VFX execution; shoot design, brand assets and strategy stay with people.

11.4 People: AI actors and digital doubles

Base image → reference input → character sheet → post-production fixes. Check likeness · scope · disclosure · records first.

11.5 Backgrounds: sets, LED walls, generated backgrounds

Choose among location · set · LED wall · chroma key+generation, but settle the background first, then light.

11.6 Elements and particles

Simulate the product and key physics; generate atmosphere and particles. Split into layers and composite.

11.7 Traditional production and AI production

Not rivals but tools for different jobs. Control the layers that carry brand assets; hand off the rest.

11.8 The Korean job market

What disappears is not jobs but the bottom rungs of the ladder. AI has become a requirement within existing roles.

11.9 Portfolio platforms

Label by process to the strictest rules and keep it the same on every channel. Inflating results is fatal.
