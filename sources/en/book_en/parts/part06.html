PART 06

Introduction

If you're still deciding on set, you're already late. A set burns hundreds of thousands of won an hour, and ten people wait on one person's decision. That is why professional sets are quiet: the decisions are already settled on paper.

Planning documents are not paperwork; they are a promise that no one will have to ask again on set. A storyboard is a device that makes ten people imagine the same frame, and the PPM is the procedure that locks every decision at the last moment when change is still cheap. In student projects too, gaps in understanding among team members must surface before the shoot day, not on it.

What I most want to stress is the one-sentence concept: this ad makes whom feel what, and in what way. If that sentence doesn't come, don't draw the storyboard. A plan that starts with pictures always collapses midway. Once the sentence is locked, everything follows from it: which cuts to drop, which music to choose, how many captions to use.

Background 9

Archetypes of story — from the Poetics to the hero's journey

From a 15-second ad to a 90-minute film, stories that grip people share recurring structures. In the Poetics, Aristotle named plot, the arrangement of events, as the most important element of tragedy. A story must be a complete action with a beginning, middle and end, and its events must connect by necessity or probability. "Reversal," where a character's fortune turns, and "recognition," where an unknown truth comes to light, move the audience most. Today's twist-ending ads follow the same principle.

In 1863, the 19th-century German writer Gustav Freytag organized dramatic structure into five stages: exposition, rising action, climax, falling action and catastrophe. "Freytag's pyramid" describes a story as a curve of tension that rises and falls. In East Asia, gi-seung-jeon-gyeol, the four-part form born of the classical Chinese quatrain, has played a similar role. Because the direction changes at the third part, the "turn," it suits short videos especially well.

In the 20th century, the mythologist Joseph Campbell argued in The Hero with a Thousand Faces (1949) that myths around the world follow a similar journey: a hero leaves the ordinary world, undergoes trials and returns transformed. Christopher Vogler reworked this into a screenwriting method in The Writer's Journey, and Syd Field popularized 3-act structure and plot points in Screenplay (1979).

Use these structures as formulas and the story turns predictable. But understand why they work and you can use them differently. The audience enters a story when they know what the character wants, tenses up when that desire is blocked, and is moved when the character makes a choice. Make three things clear — desire, obstacle, choice — and the story lives, whether in four parts or 3 acts.

The same goes for brand films. The audience accepts a story when a person, not the product, is the protagonist, and the product is the helper that gets that person over the obstacle. The planning in this PART comes down to deciding whose desire, and which desire, to set before which obstacle.

Further reading: Aristotle, Poetics · Joseph Campbell, The Hero with a Thousand Faces · Robert McKee, Story

Generating ideas: the 6 Thinking Hats

Everyone wears the same color hat and thinks in the same direction, then moves on to the next hat.

6 Thinking Hats

A thinking technique proposed by Edward de Bono. It prevents the confusion of one person juggling several perspectives at once.

White hatFacts and data

  • The target's actual viewing time?
  • Competitors' view counts? Budget?

Red hatFeelings and intuition

  • How does this idea make you feel?
  • Exciting or boring?

Black hatRisks and criticism

  • What could go wrong?
  • Scenes that can't be shot?

Yellow hatValue and optimism

  • Biggest strength?
  • If it succeeds, why?

Green hatCreativity and alternatives

  • Another way to do it?
  • What if we flip it?

Blue hatProcess control

  • Where are we now?
  • What do we need to decide?

Sample meeting agenda (60 min)

  1. 01Blue hat 5 min — state what must be decided today.
  2. 02White hat 10 min — share only data and facts. No opinions.
  3. 03Green hat 15 min — pour out ideas in quantity. No criticism.
  4. 04Yellow hat 10 min — name only each idea's strengths.
  5. 05Black hat 10 min — check the risks.
  6. 06Red hat 5 min — vote for the option your gut picks.
  7. 07Blue hat 5 min — record decisions and next steps.

Supporting ideation techniques

Five ways to strengthen a proposal

TIP

For tone and manner, three images are more precise than ten adjectives.

  1. 01Put the conclusion first. Place the one-sentence concept and a key frame on the page right after the cover.
  2. 02Speak in pictures rather than words.
  3. 03Include numbers. Specific figures such as 15 seconds, 6 cuts, 1 shoot day and 2 cast members build trust.
  4. 04Write down what's impossible up front. Presenting risks with alternatives actually raises trust.
  5. 05Narrow the options to two. Three or more delay the decision; one looks like an ultimatum.

Seven rules for collaboration

TIP

Give feedback while watching, pinned to specific times. "It's just not quite there overall" doesn't help.

  1. 01Name one decision-maker.
  2. 02Rotate roles. In student projects, trying every role once is the learning goal.
  3. 03Agree on file naming on day one. Fix the order: date, scene, cut, version.
  4. 04Set up the shared folder structure in advance. After the shoot is too late.
  5. 05Give feedback while watching, pinned to specific times.
  6. 06Set the internal deadline one day before the real one.
  7. 07Say what went well first. A team lasts because of relationships, not skills.

Note

Awards keep getting revoked over music and font copyright issues.

  • They didn't read the guidelines. Surprisingly many proposals never once use the theme's keyword in the text.
  • They wrote only what they wanted to make. A contest is a tool for achieving the organizer's goals.
  • It isn't feasible. A shoot plan too big for the budget and schedule loses points immediately.

Planning and shooting interview videos

Interviews are the most commonly commissioned format, and they expose your basics in lighting, mics, framing and editing all at once.

Preparation

TIP

Book twice the shooting time you actually need. It takes time for nerves to settle.

  1. 01Write and share the questions in advance, but use them only as a frame for conversation, not a script to read.
  2. 02Ask the subject to include the question in each answer. The questions get cut in the edit.
  3. 03Check the location in advance: quiet, with depth in the background and a usable window.
  4. 04Advise on wardrobe colors beforehand. Avoid the background's color, fine stripes and big logos.
  5. 05Book twice the shooting time you actually need.

Editing tips

B-roll

Related footage laid over the points where jump cuts occur.

  • Find the single best answer sentence first and build around it.
  • Cut verbal tics and filler, but take care not to change the meaning.
  • Lay B-roll over jump cuts. Use captions to highlight only key sentences, not everything.
  • Building in a review by the interviewee before finishing prevents disputes.

Seven concepts that drive film: from structure to emotion

Before technique, film stands on a few agreements. These seven concepts work the same in a 15-second ad and a 90-minute film.

1 Structure · 2 Screenplay · 3 Setup and payoff

25%Act 1 (setup) — at its end, the character crosses a threshold of no return
50%Act 2 (confrontation) — at its end, the character hits the lowest point
25%Act 3 (resolution) — a new balance
Chekhov's gun

A gun hung on the wall in Act 1 must be fired in Act 3. Pay off what you plant, and plant only what you will pay off.

StructureFour parts and 3 acts

  • Setup: characters and world / Development: the problem grows / Turn: change of direction / Resolution: new balance
  • The shorter the video, the more one moment of the "turn" decides the whole

ScreenplayWrite only what is seen and heard

  • Scene heading (place · time), action notes, dialogue
  • Don't write inner thoughts; show them in action
  • 1 page = about 1 minute of screen time

Setup and payoffMake a promise and keep it

  • Plant setups so naturally the audience overlooks them
  • Payoffs clear enough to bring back the earlier scene

4 Actor and subject · 5 Mise-en-scène · 6 Acting · 7 Emotion

Actor and subjectRead by size · position · light · direction

  • Small at the edge: isolation
  • Large in the center: control

Mise-en-scène"Put in the scene"

  • Placing actors, space, props, costume, light, color
  • Shows relationships and power in one frame. Pairs with montage

ActingSmaller reads bigger

  • In close-up, eye movement and breath are enough
  • Give actions and goals, not emotions
  • Reaction is half of acting

EmotionThe audience's part

  • The audience does not copy a character's emotion
  • Design the order: desire · obstacle · choice

Background 10

Design thinking and the language of feeling

Setting a world with adjectives may look like pure instinct, but it rests on methods long refined in design research and psychology. In The Sciences of the Artificial (1969), Herbert Simon defined design as "action that turns the current state into a better one," widening it from a branch of art into a method of problem solving. Later, IDEO and the Stanford d.school popularized the stages of empathize, define, ideate, prototype, and test, and the term "design thinking" came into wide use.

The classic study using adjectives as a measuring tool is psychologist Charles Osgood's semantic differential. In The Measurement of Meaning (1957), he had people score their impressions on pairs of opposite adjectives such as "good-bad," "strong-weak," and "fast-slow." Placing adjectives on sensory axes in section 2.13 applies exactly this principle.

In the 1970s the Japanese engineer Mitsuo Nagamachi systematized "Kansei engineering," which gathers users' feelings about a product as adjectives and turns them into concrete design elements. It translates the vague sense of a "sporty car" into design values such as body lines, seat height, and dashboard color. The tone-and-manner chart, which turns adjectives into color, light, lens, and cutting pace in video, has the same structure.

The adjective method is especially useful because it aligns the team's language. The director's "warm," the DP's "warm," and the editor's "warm" can all differ. A mood board and a tone-and-manner chart bring these differences out before the shoot and get the team to agree. Ask first-time viewers of the finished piece which adjectives come to mind, and you can check whether the intended world came through.

Design thinking, in the end, is refusing to leave creativity to chance and making it a repeated cycle of observation, definition, and experiment. Three adjectives are where that cycle starts and where you return when lost.

Further reading: Herbert Simon, The Sciences of the Artificial · Tim Brown, Change by Design · Charles Osgood et al., The Measurement of Meaning

2.13

Design thinking that builds a world from adjective keywords

Set the goal with adjectives (how) rather than nouns (what), and your choices of color, light, lens, pace, and sound line up all at once.

A good video sits inside one world from the first cut. Adjective keywords are the fastest tool for getting the whole team to build that world in the same direction.

The five stages of design thinking

TIP

Combinations with tension (cold · still · tender) make a more distinctive world than ordinary ones (bright · cheerful · exciting).

01EmpathizeObserve · interview the target's viewing context and mood. Collect their adjectives
02DefinePick 3 of the collected adjectives
03IdeateA mood board of 10 images per adjective. Plot them on sensory axes (cold-warm, static-dynamic)
04PrototypeTurn it into a tone-and-manner chart and 3 test cuts. Color · light · lens · camera · editing · sound · don'ts on one page
05TestShow the test cuts to 5 first-time viewers and ask for adjectives. It works if 2 or more intended ones come up

Adjectives into production language

StillA frame at rest

  • Static shots, long takes
  • Ambience-led, minimal dialogue

SharpHard-edged light

  • Hard light, wide lens up close
  • Short cuts, metallic SFX

TenderFaces up close

  • Diffused window light, one warm color
  • Detail inserts, such as hands

Questions to consider

What is my project's one-sentence concept? Would it still work with a rival's logo on it?
If it would, you have failed to differentiate. Look again for what rivals can't say, in a form you can prove visually.
Have you put off any decision until shoot day? What changes if you make it now?
A change on shoot day costs 20 times what it costs at the concept stage. Send meeting materials 48 hours ahead, share decisions in writing the same day, and always secure a 10% contingency and one spare shoot day.
